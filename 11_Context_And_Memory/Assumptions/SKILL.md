# Skill: Assumptions

## Purpose

This skill identifies, records, evaluates, validates, and monitors assumptions that influence executive decisions, initiatives, plans, forecasts, recommendations, risks, and expected outcomes.

It helps leadership understand:

- What is being treated as true without complete confirmation.
- Why the assumption is necessary.
- What evidence supports or contradicts it.
- Which decisions and plans rely upon it.
- What happens if it is incorrect.
- How and when it should be validated.
- Whether contingency or risk management is required.
- When the assumption should be revised, invalidated, or retired.

An assumption is a statement treated as true for planning or decision-making despite incomplete evidence. It must not be presented as a confirmed fact.

## Core concepts

### Fact

A statement supported by sufficient current and reliable evidence.

### Assumption

A statement treated as true for planning or decision-making but not yet fully confirmed.

### Hypothesis

A testable explanation or proposition that has not yet been established.

### Constraint

A known limitation or requirement affecting available choices.

### Dependency

An input, decision, resource, or condition required from another party or activity.

### Risk

An uncertain future event or condition that may affect an objective.

### Issue

A condition or event that has already occurred.

These categories should not be used interchangeably.

## Core activities

### 1. Identify assumptions

Extract explicit and implicit assumptions from:

- Charters.
- Business cases.
- Plans.
- Forecasts.
- Recommendations.
- Meeting notes.
- Decisions.
- Financial models.
- Resource plans.
- Architecture and technical designs.
- Risk assessments.
- Stakeholder communications.
- Vendor proposals.
- Research or market analysis.

Common signals include:

- “We expect…”
- “We believe…”
- “It is likely…”
- “This depends on…”
- “The plan assumes…”
- “We anticipate…”
- “Based on current information…”

Do not invent assumptions that are not reasonably supported by the source context.

### 2. Normalize the assumption statement

Write each assumption so it is:

- Specific.
- Testable when possible.
- Time-bound.
- Connected to a decision or outcome.
- Free of unnecessary ambiguity.

A useful structure is:

> For planning purposes, we assume that [condition] will be true by [date], enabling [decision, deliverable, or outcome].

Preserve the original source wording when material.

### 3. Distinguish assumptions from other record types

Determine whether the statement is actually:

- A verified fact.
- An assumption.
- A hypothesis.
- A constraint.
- A dependency.
- A risk.
- An issue.
- A decision.
- A commitment.

If a condition has already failed, it may need to become an issue. If uncertainty creates material future exposure, it may need to become a risk.

### 4. Assess evidence

For each assumption:

- Identify supporting evidence.
- Identify contradictory evidence.
- Assess source authority.
- Assess source recency.
- Assess corroboration.
- Identify bias or limitations.
- Assign a confidence level.
- Explain the confidence assessment.

Do not imply certainty merely because several documents repeat the same unsupported assumption.

### 5. Determine materiality

Assess:

- Which decisions rely on the assumption.
- Which deliverables or milestones depend on it.
- Financial or resource implications.
- Strategic or operational effect.
- Regulatory, clinical, legal, security, privacy, or reputational consequences.
- Number of initiatives affected.
- Time available to respond.
- Reversibility if incorrect.

Focus leadership attention on assumptions whose failure would materially change a decision, plan, or outcome.

### 6. Define validation

For every material assumption:

- State the validation question.
- Identify the evidence required.
- Select the validation method.
- Assign an owner.
- Set a deadline.
- Identify the decision or milestone requiring validation.
- Define success and failure criteria.
- Establish a follow-up date.

Validation may involve:

- Data analysis.
- Stakeholder confirmation.
- Pilot or experiment.
- Technical test.
- Financial analysis.
- Legal or policy review.
- Vendor confirmation.
- Governance review.
- Operational evidence.

### 7. Monitor assumption status

Track assumptions through:

- Proposed.
- Unvalidated.
- Partially supported.
- Supported.
- Validated.
- Challenged.
- Invalidated.
- Replaced.
- Expired.
- Retired.

Review assumptions when:

- New evidence appears.
- Strategy changes.
- Scope changes.
- A milestone approaches.
- A risk trigger occurs.
- A decision is required.
- The assumption reaches its expiration date.

### 8. Convert material uncertainty into risk

Recommend conversion to a risk when:

- The assumption cannot be validated in time.
- There is a meaningful probability it is wrong.
- The effect of being wrong is material.
- Mitigation or contingency is necessary.
- Monitoring indicators can be defined.

Do not treat every assumption as a risk. Low-impact planning assumptions may simply require periodic review.

### 9. Detect conflicts

Identify:

- Teams relying on conflicting assumptions.
- Different baselines or time horizons.
- Assumptions inconsistent with approved policy or strategy.
- Assumptions contradicted by current evidence.
- Duplicate assumptions.
- Assumptions presented as confirmed facts.
- Decisions using outdated assumptions.

### 10. Define contingency actions

For critical assumptions:

- Identify the invalidation trigger.
- Define the contingency.
- Identify the owner.
- Establish required resources.
- Identify the decision authority.
- Define activation timing.
- Identify affected stakeholders.
- Establish communication and escalation requirements.

### 11. Preserve history

Maintain:

- Original wording.
- Revisions.
- Evidence changes.
- Confidence changes.
- Validation results.
- Decisions affected.
- Risk conversions.
- Contingencies.
- Retirement reason.

Do not overwrite history when an assumption changes.

## Decision rules

- Clearly label assumptions as assumptions.
- Distinguish assumptions from facts, constraints, dependencies, risks, and issues.
- Connect every material assumption to the decision or outcome it affects.
- Record evidence and confidence.
- Set a validation owner and date.
- Do not treat repetition as corroboration when sources rely on the same original claim.
- Do not maintain an invalidated assumption solely to preserve the current plan.
- Convert material uncertainty into a risk when appropriate.
- Preserve original and revised assumptions.
- Do not infer institutional acceptance from silence.
- Clearly separate analytical recommendations from approved decisions.

## Quality standards

A strong assumption record must be:

- Explicit.
- Specific.
- Testable.
- Time-bound.
- Evidence-aware.
- Confidence-aware.
- Decision-linked.
- Risk-aware.
- Traceable.
- Reviewable.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not fabricate assumptions, evidence, confidence, owners, validation results, or institutional positions.
- Do not present assumptions as verified facts.
- Do not make definitive legal, clinical, security, privacy, regulatory, financial, or policy conclusions.
- Do not automatically change decisions, forecasts, risks, or plans.
- Do not disclose confidential or restricted information to unauthorized audiences.
- Flag incomplete, stale, conflicting, or weak evidence.
- Require human and specialized validation for consequential assumptions.

## Human role

The assumption owner, initiative lead, decision owner, subject-matter expert, risk owner, governance partner, and authorized executive must:

- Confirm the assumption wording.
- Validate evidence.
- Approve the validation method.
- Determine acceptable confidence.
- Decide whether work may proceed.
- Approve contingencies.
- Convert assumptions into risks or issues when appropriate.
- Authorize revisions, invalidation, or retirement.
