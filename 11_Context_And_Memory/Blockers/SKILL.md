# Skill: Blockers

## Purpose

This skill identifies, validates, analyzes, monitors, and supports resolution of blockers preventing work, decisions, approvals, deliverables, milestones, or outcomes from proceeding.

It helps leadership understand:

- What is unable to proceed.
- What condition is preventing progress.
- Who controls or can resolve that condition.
- What initiatives, decisions, milestones, or stakeholders are affected.
- What happens if resolution is delayed.
- What action, decision, resource, or escalation is required.
- Whether a workaround or contingency exists.
- When the blocker has been genuinely resolved.

A blocker is a current condition that prevents required work or a decision from proceeding. It is stronger than a general concern, risk, delay, or inconvenience.

## Core distinctions

### Blocker

A present condition that prevents work or a decision from proceeding.

### Risk

An uncertain future event or condition that may affect an objective.

### Issue

A current problem affecting performance but not necessarily stopping progress.

### Dependency

A required input or condition from another party or activity.

### Constraint

A known limitation within which the work must operate.

### Action

A task assigned to a person or role.

These categories should not be used interchangeably.

## Core activities

### 1. Validate the blocker

Before classifying an item as a blocker, confirm:

- Specific work or decision cannot proceed.
- The preventing condition exists now.
- The blocked work is required.
- The cause is documented or can be validated.
- The owner and affected outcome are identifiable.

If work can continue with manageable difficulty, classify the matter as an issue, risk, dependency, or constraint rather than a blocker.

### 2. Normalize the blocker statement

Use a clear structure:

> [Work, decision, or milestone] cannot proceed because [specific condition], requiring [input, action, decision, approval, or resource] from [owner] by [date].

Avoid vague statements such as:

- “Waiting on leadership.”
- “IT is blocking us.”
- “Resource problem.”
- “Approval needed.”

Specify exactly what is missing and who can resolve it.

### 3. Identify blocked work

Determine:

- What stopped.
- What can continue.
- What milestone is affected.
- Whether the work is on the critical path.
- What downstream work is affected.
- Which other initiatives are affected.
- Whether partial progress is possible.

### 4. Analyze the cause

Distinguish:

- Immediate cause.
- Underlying cause.
- Contributing conditions.
- Missing decision authority.
- Missing input or acceptance criteria.
- Resource or capacity conflict.
- Technical or data limitation.
- Governance or approval requirement.
- External-provider delay.

Do not make unsupported claims about fault, motives, performance, or resistance.

### 5. Assess impact and urgency

Evaluate:

- Current impact.
- Forecast impact.
- Cost of delay.
- Critical-path effect.
- Time available.
- Number of initiatives affected.
- Strategic, operational, financial, clinical, security, privacy, legal, regulatory, stakeholder, or reputational consequences.
- Reversibility.

Severity reflects consequence. Urgency reflects how soon action is needed.

### 6. Identify the resolution owner

Differentiate:

- Person experiencing the blocker.
- Owner of the blocked work.
- Owner of the preventing condition.
- Person authorized to resolve it.
- Sponsor or executive able to intervene.

Do not assign the blocker solely to the person reporting it when another party controls resolution.

### 7. Develop resolution options

Possible responses include:

- Supply the missing input.
- Clarify requirements.
- Make a pending decision.
- Complete a review or approval.
- Allocate resources.
- Resolve a priority conflict.
- Use an alternate provider.
- Resequence work.
- Implement a temporary workaround.
- Reduce or clarify scope.
- Activate contingency.
- Escalate.

For each option, assess:

- Time.
- Cost.
- Feasibility.
- Risk.
- Required authority.
- Effect on downstream work.

### 8. Select proportionate escalation

Escalate to the lowest level that can resolve the blocker.

Potential levels include:

- Owner coordination.
- Program-lead intervention.
- Sponsor intervention.
- Cross-functional governance review.
- Executive decision.
- Specialized incident, risk, legal, clinical, privacy, security, or regulatory process.

Escalation should request a specific resolution—not merely report frustration.

### 9. Monitor blocker lifecycle

Track:

- Reported.
- Validated.
- Assigned.
- Resolution in progress.
- Workaround active.
- Escalated.
- Resolved.
- Closure validated.

Record status changes, decisions, revised dates, and evidence.

### 10. Validate resolution and closure

A blocker is resolved when:

- The preventing condition has been removed or formally bypassed.
- Blocked work can proceed.
- Required input, decision, approval, or resource exists.
- The authorized owner confirms resolution.
- Residual actions and risks are recorded.

Do not close a blocker merely because a meeting occurred or a resolution plan was created.

### 11. Identify systemic patterns

Across blockers, identify:

- Recurring decision delays.
- Approval bottlenecks.
- Resource conflicts.
- Vendor or procurement delays.
- Technology or data constraints.
- Unclear ownership.
- Missing acceptance criteria.
- Governance-capacity problems.
- Repeated workarounds.
- Blockers affecting multiple initiatives.

## Decision rules

- Confirm that progress is actually prevented.
- Identify the exact blocked work.
- Identify who controls the resolution.
- Distinguish blockers from risks, issues, dependencies, constraints, and actions.
- Do not infer fault or poor performance.
- Separate immediate cause from root cause.
- Assess impact and urgency separately.
- Escalate to the lowest level able to resolve the matter.
- Request a specific decision, input, approval, or resource.
- Do not mark resolved without evidence that work can proceed.
- Preserve the history of workarounds and escalations.
- Clearly separate recommendations from authorized decisions.

## Quality standards

A strong blocker record must be:

- Specific.
- Current.
- Impact-linked.
- Correctly owned.
- Resolution-oriented.
- Time-bound.
- Evidence-based.
- Escalation-ready.
- Traceable.
- Closure-validated.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not fabricate blockers, causes, ownership, impact, decisions, or resolution evidence.
- Do not make personnel, performance, disciplinary, or motive judgments.
- Do not automatically escalate, contact stakeholders, assign work, or change priorities.
- Do not bypass required governance or specialized processes.
- Do not disclose confidential, personnel, clinical, legal, vendor, financial, security, or privacy information to unauthorized audiences.
- Flag stale, conflicting, incomplete, or low-confidence information.
- Require human review before consequential escalation or closure.

## Human role

The blocked-work owner, resolution owner, program lead, sponsor, dependency owner, governance partner, specialized reviewer, or authorized executive must:

- Confirm the blocker.
- Validate cause and impact.
- Assign the resolution owner.
- Select and authorize the resolution.
- Approve workarounds.
- Resolve priority or resource conflicts.
- Authorize escalation.
- Confirm resolution and closure.
