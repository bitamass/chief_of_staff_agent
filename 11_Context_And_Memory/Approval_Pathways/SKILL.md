# Skill: Approval Pathways

## Purpose

This skill identifies and explains the reviews, consultations, decisions, evidence, sequencing, conditions, and ongoing obligations that may apply to a proposed initiative, technology, data use, procurement, research activity, operational change, policy, communication, or organizational decision.

It helps leadership and request owners understand:

* Which reviews and approvals may be required.
* Why each review appears applicable.
* Who prepares, reviews, and decides.
* What evidence is needed.
* Which activities can occur in parallel.
* Which approvals depend on prior reviews.
* What conditions must be satisfied before implementation.
* When renewal or re-review is required.
* What remains uncertain and requires authoritative human interpretation.

The skill supports routing and preparation. It does not approve, reject, authorize, certify, waive, or provide definitive legal or regulatory determinations.

## Core concepts

### Consultation

A person or function provides advice or expertise but does not necessarily possess decision authority.

### Review

An authorized function evaluates evidence against defined criteria and may provide findings, recommendations, conditions, or required remediation.

### Approval

An authorized person or body grants permission within a documented scope and under stated conditions.

### Authorization to implement

A specific decision allowing approved work to proceed. Completion of an advisory review does not necessarily provide implementation authorization.

### Conditional approval

Approval granted subject to documented requirements that remain enforceable.

### Exception

Authorized deviation from an applicable requirement under a defined exception process.

### Renewal or re-review

A new review required because approval expires, conditions change, or the request undergoes a material change.

## Core activities

### 1. Classify the request

Determine:

* Request type.
* Scope.
* Intended outcome.
* Current phase.
* Data involved.
* Technology involved.
* People or operations affected.
* External parties.
* Risk and obligation categories.
* Desired implementation date.

Do not route solely from the request title.

### 2. Identify authoritative requirements

Review current approved:

* Policies.
* Standards.
* Delegations of authority.
* Governance charters.
* Laws and regulations.
* Contracts and grant conditions.
* Review procedures.
* Existing precedents.

Record the source, version, effective date, and owner.

Do not treat informal practice or an old approval as current authority without validation.

### 3. Determine potentially applicable reviews

Evaluate whether the request triggers review involving:

* Strategy and sponsorship.
* Architecture.
* Security.
* Privacy.
* Data governance.
* Clinical operations.
* Research.
* Legal and regulatory requirements.
* Finance.
* Procurement and vendors.
* Accessibility.
* Records management.
* Policy.
* Workforce or labor.
* Communications.
* Responsible AI, ethics, or model governance.
* Board or formal institutional governance.

For every recommended review, explain its apparent basis.

### 4. Distinguish review from approval

Identify:

* Who advises.
* Who reviews.
* Who recommends.
* Who approves.
* Who authorizes implementation.
* Who monitors conditions.
* Who may accept residual risk.

Do not state that a reviewer has approval authority unless the source documents that authority.

### 5. Build the pathway

For each stage, identify:

* Entry criteria.
* Required materials.
* Responsible preparer.
* Reviewer or decision body.
* Decision criteria.
* Expected lead time.
* Exit criteria.
* Dependencies.
* Conditions.
* Escalation path.

Show which reviews may proceed concurrently and which require prior completion.

### 6. Identify the minimum sufficient pathway

Avoid unnecessary duplication.

Determine:

* Whether an existing approval remains valid.
* Whether the proposal represents a material change.
* Whether one review satisfies multiple requirements.
* Whether a limited demonstration or pilot follows a different pathway.
* Whether production use triggers additional review.
* Whether only consultation, rather than approval, is required.

The goal is a complete and proportionate pathway—not the maximum possible number of reviews.

### 7. Prepare the evidence checklist

Identify the materials needed to enable a responsible decision.

Do not mark the package complete merely because files exist. Determine whether they contain the required information and evidence.

### 8. Track decisions and conditions

Preserve:

* Decision.
* Scope.
* Decision-maker.
* Date.
* Conditions.
* Exceptions.
* Expiration.
* Responsible owners.
* Evidence of condition fulfillment.
* Changes since approval.

Do not summarize a conditional or limited approval as unrestricted approval.

### 9. Detect material changes

Potential triggers for re-review include changes to:

* Scope.
* Purpose.
* Users.
* Data.
* Data classification.
* Platform or architecture.
* Vendor.
* Model or automation behavior.
* Production connection.
* Level of autonomy.
* Geography.
* Risk.
* Retention.
* External sharing.
* Operational impact.

The governing policy or decision owner must determine whether a change is legally or institutionally material.

### 10. Identify pathway gaps

Surface:

* Missing authority.
* Unclear ownership.
* Missing evidence.
* Conflicting requirements.
* Duplicate review.
* Approval represented too broadly.
* Expired approval.
* Unresolved conditions.
* Implementation occurring before authorization.
* Ongoing obligations without owners.

### 11. Recommend next steps

Every recommendation should state:

* Action.
* Owner.
* Reviewer or decision-maker.
* Evidence required.
* Deadline.
* Dependency.
* Escalation condition.

## Decision rules

* Use current authoritative sources.
* State why each review appears applicable.
* Distinguish consultation, review, recommendation, approval, and authorization.
* Do not infer approval authority from job title alone.
* Do not treat silence or meeting attendance as approval.
* Preserve approval scope, conditions, and expiration.
* Do not represent conditional approval as full approval.
* Identify changes that may require re-review.
* Avoid unnecessary duplicate reviews.
* Do not bypass mandatory processes because of urgency.
* Clearly label uncertain routing.
* Route legal, regulatory, clinical, security, privacy, financial, labor, or research interpretations to authorized specialists.
* Separate recommended pathways from confirmed institutional requirements.

## Quality standards

A strong approval-pathway output must be:

* Authoritative.
* Current.
* Proportionate.
* Sequenced.
* Role-specific.
* Evidence-based.
* Condition-aware.
* Traceable.
* Transparent about uncertainty.
* Actionable.

## Safeguards and limitations

* Do not approve, reject, authorize, certify, or waive requirements.
* Do not provide definitive legal, regulatory, clinical, research, privacy, security, labor, financial, or policy determinations.
* Do not invent decision authority, policies, approvals, or precedents.
* Do not bypass protected or mandatory workflows.
* Do not expose confidential, privileged, clinical, research, personnel, financial, security, or procurement information to unauthorized audiences.
* Do not automatically submit requests, contact reviewers, or schedule approvals.
* Flag outdated, conflicting, incomplete, or low-confidence information.
* Require human and specialized confirmation before consequential use.

## Human role

The request owner, executive sponsor, policy owner, governance lead, legal counsel, security and privacy partners, data owner, research or clinical reviewers, finance and procurement partners, and authorized decision-makers must:

* Confirm request classification.
* Determine which requirements legally or institutionally apply.
* Validate the pathway.
* Review evidence.
* Make authorized decisions.
* Establish conditions.
* Approve exceptions.
* Determine whether changes require re-review.
* Confirm implementation authorization and ongoing compliance.
