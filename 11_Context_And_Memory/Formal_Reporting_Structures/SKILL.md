# Skill: Formal Reporting Structures

## Purpose

This skill identifies, organizes, and explains formal organizational reporting relationships, governance structures, decision rights, delegated authorities, and reporting obligations.

It helps leadership understand:

- Who formally reports to whom.
- Which relationships are managerial, functional, programmatic, governance-based, or informational.
- Who prepares, reviews, recommends, approves, and implements decisions.
- Which governance bodies oversee specific matters.
- What reports must be produced, by whom, for whom, and when.
- How risks, issues, decisions, and exceptions are escalated.
- Where authority, accountability, or reporting relationships are unclear or conflicting.
- Whether organizational records reflect current approved structures.

The skill documents and analyzes formal structures. It does not change organizational reporting lines, assign authority, interpret employment obligations definitively, or create new governance arrangements.

## Core concepts

### Direct reporting relationship

A formal managerial relationship in which one role reports to another role with defined supervisory authority.

### Functional reporting relationship

A relationship in which a person or team follows the standards, priorities, or direction of a functional leader while possibly reporting managerially elsewhere.

### Matrix reporting relationship

A structure in which a role has responsibilities to more than one leader, such as a people manager and a program or functional leader.

### Programmatic reporting relationship

A relationship established for the duration of a program, initiative, project, or workstream.

### Governance reporting relationship

A formal obligation to provide information, recommendations, decisions, risks, or performance information to an oversight or decision body.

### Informational relationship

A requirement to keep a person or group informed without transferring formal authority.

### Delegated authority

Authority formally transferred within documented limits, conditions, and timeframes.

### Escalation relationship

The approved pathway used when an issue, decision, risk, conflict, or exception cannot be resolved at the current level.

## Core activities

### 1. Establish the authoritative scope

Confirm:

- Organization or area being mapped.
- Effective date.
- Applicable organizational records.
- Relevant policies.
- Governance charters.
- Delegations of authority.
- Contracts or regulatory requirements.
- Current versus future-state structure.

Use authoritative and current sources whenever possible.

### 2. Identify organizational units and roles

For each unit and role:

- Record name and purpose.
- Identify parent unit.
- Identify accountable leader.
- Identify formal reports.
- Identify responsibilities.
- Identify decision and budget authority.
- Identify effective dates.
- Identify the source.

Do not infer formal authority from visibility, meeting attendance, seniority, or informal influence.

### 3. Classify reporting relationships

Classify each relationship as:

- Direct managerial.
- Functional.
- Matrix.
- Programmatic.
- Financial.
- Governance.
- Regulatory.
- Advisory.
- Oversight.
- Contractual.
- Operational.
- Informational.

A person may participate in several relationship types. Keep each relationship and its authority distinct.

### 4. Map governance bodies

For each governing, advisory, or oversight body:

- Confirm the charter.
- Identify the sponsoring authority.
- Identify members.
- Identify decision rights.
- Identify reporting obligations.
- Identify escalation authority.
- Identify meeting and record requirements.
- Confirm effective dates.

Do not treat a working group or advisory body as a decision-making authority without evidence.

### 5. Map decision rights

For major decision categories, identify:

- Who prepares the decision.
- Who is responsible.
- Who must be consulted.
- Who reviews.
- Who recommends.
- Who approves.
- Who implements.
- Who accepts residual risk.
- Who receives the final decision.
- Where unresolved matters escalate.

Distinguish recommendation authority from approval authority.

### 6. Map formal reporting obligations

For each required report:

- Identify purpose.
- Identify owner.
- Identify recipient.
- Identify frequency.
- Identify submission deadline.
- Identify required content.
- Identify approval.
- Identify records and retention requirements.
- Identify consequence of failure.

A recurring report should exist to support a decision, obligation, oversight function, or accountable relationship.

### 7. Analyze matrix relationships

Determine:

- Who sets day-to-day priorities.
- Who allocates resources.
- Who approves work.
- Who manages performance.
- Who resolves conflicting priorities.
- Who approves leave or availability.
- Who receives status and escalation.

Surface unresolved overlaps rather than silently selecting an authority.

### 8. Identify conflicts and gaps

Detect:

- Multiple roles claiming approval authority.
- Responsibilities without authority.
- Authority without defined accountability.
- Reports without owners.
- Decisions without escalation paths.
- Governance bodies with outdated or missing charters.
- Expired delegations.
- Informal practices inconsistent with documented structures.
- Organizational changes not reflected in current records.
- Duplicate or unnecessary reporting requirements.

### 9. Preserve effective dates and history

Formal structures change.

Maintain:

- Effective dates.
- Previous relationships.
- Acting and interim assignments.
- Delegation periods.
- Approved reorganizations.
- Transitional reporting arrangements.
- Source versions.

Do not overwrite historical reporting context when structures change.

### 10. Recommend clarification

For each ambiguity:

- State the issue.
- Identify conflicting records.
- Explain the consequence.
- Identify the authority able to clarify it.
- Recommend documentation or a decision.
- Identify an interim approach, when necessary.
- Set a validation date.

The skill may recommend clarification but must not establish authority.

## Decision rules

- Use current authoritative sources.
- Distinguish formal authority from informal influence.
- Distinguish managerial, functional, matrix, programmatic, and governance relationships.
- Do not infer authority from job title alone.
- Do not infer approval from attendance or participation.
- Preserve effective dates and source versions.
- Identify interim and delegated authority explicitly.
- Do not treat recommendations as decisions.
- Do not treat advisory bodies as approving bodies without evidence.
- Connect reports to a recipient and decision or obligation.
- Clearly label conflicts, gaps, assumptions, and low-confidence relationships.

## Quality standards

A strong formal-reporting-structure output must be:

- Authoritative.
- Current.
- Role-specific.
- Relationship-specific.
- Decision-rights aware.
- Time-bound.
- Traceable.
- Conflict-aware.
- Reviewable.
- Actionable.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not invent reporting relationships, authority, delegation, governance bodies, or obligations.
- Do not modify organizational structures or decision rights.
- Do not make employment, labor, legal, regulatory, or policy determinations.
- Do not disclose confidential personnel or organizational information to unauthorized audiences.
- Do not automatically contact individuals, assign roles, or publish an organizational chart.
- Flag outdated, conflicting, incomplete, or low-confidence records.
- Require HR, governance, policy, legal, leadership, or other authorized review where appropriate.

## Human role

The organizational leader, people manager, HR partner, governance lead, policy owner, legal partner, program or portfolio owner, and authorized executive must:

- Confirm organizational structures.
- Validate reporting relationships.
- Approve delegations.
- Clarify decision rights.
- Resolve matrix conflicts.
- Confirm governance charters.
- Validate formal reporting obligations.
- Approve and communicate organizational changes.
