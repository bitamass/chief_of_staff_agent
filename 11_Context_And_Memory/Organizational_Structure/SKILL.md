# Skill: Organizational Structure

## Purpose

This skill identifies, organizes, and explains an organization’s formal units, leadership roles, reporting relationships, responsibilities, decision rights, governance bodies, shared services, and initiative overlays.

It helps leadership understand:

- How the organization is formally arranged.
- Which units and roles own particular responsibilities.
- Who reports to whom.
- Who possesses decision, budget, operational, or governance authority.
- How shared services and cross-functional relationships operate.
- How initiatives overlay the permanent organization.
- Where responsibility, authority, or capability gaps exist.
- How the current structure differs from prior or approved future structures.

The skill documents and analyzes organizational structure. It does not redesign the organization, assign personnel, establish reporting relationships, or change formal authority.

## Core concepts

### Organizational unit

A formally established division, department, office, center, program, service, or other organizational component.

### Role

A defined set of responsibilities and authority that may be occupied by an individual.

### Position

A formally established job or appointment within the organization.

### Reporting relationship

A formal managerial, functional, matrix, programmatic, governance, operational, or informational relationship.

### Governance body

A board, council, committee, steering group, advisory group, or other body with documented oversight, decision, approval, recommendation, or advisory responsibilities.

### Shared service

A function that provides services or capabilities to multiple organizational units.

### Initiative overlay

A temporary or cross-functional arrangement created to deliver a program, project, or strategic initiative.

### Formal structure

Structure established through authorized organizational records, charters, delegations, policies, or decisions.

### Informal network

Working relationships and influence that exist in practice but do not necessarily carry formal authority.

Formal structure and informal influence must not be confused.

## Core activities

### 1. Define the organizational scope

Confirm:

- Organization or unit in scope.
- Purpose of the analysis.
- Effective date.
- Current-state, historical, or future-state focus.
- Intended audience.
- Required level of detail.
- Authoritative sources.
- Confidentiality restrictions.

### 2. Identify authoritative sources

Use current approved:

- Organization charts.
- Position and role documentation.
- Governance charters.
- Delegations of authority.
- Policies and procedures.
- Leadership announcements.
- Reorganization decisions.
- Program charters.
- Shared-service agreements.
- Contracts and legal records.

Record version, effective date, owner, and approval status.

### 3. Map organizational units

For each unit:

- Identify parent and subordinate units.
- Identify leader.
- Define purpose.
- Define responsibilities.
- Define scope.
- Identify decision and budget authority.
- Identify effective date.
- Link to the authoritative source.

Do not create formal units from informal team names without evidence.

### 4. Map roles and positions

For each material role:

- Identify organizational placement.
- Identify formal reports.
- Identify direct reports.
- Identify responsibilities.
- Identify authority.
- Identify appointment status.
- Identify delegation and backup.
- Identify effective and expiration dates.

Do not infer formal authority from seniority, visibility, meeting attendance, or influence.

### 5. Map reporting relationships

Classify relationships as:

- Direct managerial.
- Functional.
- Matrix.
- Programmatic.
- Governance.
- Financial.
- Operational.
- Advisory.
- Oversight.
- Contractual.
- Informational.

Keep relationship types separate. A functional or program lead may direct work without being the formal people manager.

### 6. Map responsibilities

For each unit and role:

- Identify work owned.
- Identify services provided.
- Identify systems, data, policies, risks, or budgets owned.
- Identify stakeholders served.
- Identify external obligations.
- Identify shared responsibilities.
- Identify areas of overlap.

Separate accountability from participation.

### 7. Map decision rights

For major decision categories:

- Identify who prepares.
- Identify who is responsible.
- Identify consultees.
- Identify recommending authority.
- Identify reviewer.
- Identify approving authority.
- Identify implementation authority.
- Identify risk-acceptance authority.
- Identify escalation.

Do not treat recommendation or consultation as approval.

### 8. Map governance bodies

For each body:

- Confirm charter.
- Confirm sponsor.
- Confirm chair and membership.
- Identify mandate.
- Identify decision or advisory authority.
- Identify reporting destination.
- Identify escalation.
- Identify records requirements.
- Confirm effective date.

A working group should not be represented as an approving body without documented authority.

### 9. Map shared services

Identify:

- Service provider.
- Receiving units.
- Service owner.
- Intake process.
- Priority-setting authority.
- Funding model.
- Capacity limits.
- Service expectations.
- Escalation route.

Surface unclear service ownership or conflicting priority authority.

### 10. Overlay programs and initiatives

For each major initiative:

- Identify sponsor.
- Identify accountable owner.
- Identify program lead.
- Identify participating units.
- Identify temporary reporting or coordination relationships.
- Identify governance body.
- Identify operational owner.
- Identify transition and closure arrangements.

Do not confuse temporary initiative roles with permanent organizational authority.

### 11. Detect gaps and overlaps

Identify:

- Responsibilities without owners.
- Multiple units claiming the same accountability.
- Authority disconnected from responsibility.
- Duplicate functions.
- Conflicting reporting relationships.
- Missing escalation paths.
- Governance bodies with overlapping mandates.
- Critical skills or functions without sufficient coverage.
- Informal arrangements operating without documentation.
- Organizational records that do not reflect current operations.

### 12. Preserve organizational history

When structures change:

- Preserve prior structure.
- Record approved change.
- Record effective date.
- Identify responsibilities transferred.
- Identify roles and units affected.
- Record interim arrangements.
- Identify documents requiring update.

Do not overwrite historical context.

### 13. Recommend clarification

For every structural ambiguity:

- State the gap.
- Identify affected units or roles.
- Explain the organizational consequence.
- Identify the authoritative decision-maker.
- Recommend clarification or documentation.
- Establish an interim approach when appropriate.
- Set a validation date.

The skill may identify and recommend; it must not establish the organizational structure.

## Decision rules

- Use current authoritative sources.
- Distinguish units, roles, positions, governance bodies, and initiatives.
- Distinguish formal authority from informal influence.
- Distinguish managerial, functional, matrix, programmatic, and governance relationships.
- Do not infer authority from title alone.
- Preserve effective dates and history.
- Identify acting, interim, and delegated authority.
- Do not treat advisory bodies as approving authorities without evidence.
- Separate permanent structure from temporary initiative overlays.
- Label conflicts, assumptions, and low-confidence information.
- Clearly distinguish analytical recommendations from authorized organizational decisions.

## Quality standards

A strong organizational-structure output must be:

- Authoritative.
- Current.
- Hierarchical.
- Responsibility-aware.
- Decision-rights aware.
- Relationship-specific.
- Time-bound.
- Conflict-aware.
- Traceable.
- Reviewable.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not fabricate organizational units, roles, incumbents, responsibilities, relationships, or authority.
- Do not redesign the organization or assign people.
- Do not make employment, performance, labor, legal, regulatory, or policy determinations.
- Do not disclose confidential personnel or organizational information to unauthorized audiences.
- Do not infer protected or sensitive personal information.
- Do not automatically publish organizational charts or contact individuals.
- Flag stale, conflicting, incomplete, or low-confidence records.
- Require HR, governance, policy, legal, leadership, or other authorized review.

## Human role

The organizational leader, people manager, HR partner, governance lead, policy owner, legal partner, program or portfolio owner, and authorized executive must:

- Confirm organizational units and roles.
- Validate reporting relationships.
- Approve delegations.
- Clarify decision rights.
- Resolve structural conflicts.
- Confirm governance charters.
- Approve organizational changes.
- Authorize official publication or use.
