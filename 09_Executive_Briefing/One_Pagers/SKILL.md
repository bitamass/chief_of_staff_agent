# Skill: One Pagers

## Purpose

This skill converts approved information about an initiative, proposal, decision, risk, issue, capability, or opportunity into a concise executive one-pager.

It helps leadership quickly understand:

- What the matter is.
- Why it matters.
- Why action may be needed now.
- What decision or support is requested.
- What options and trade-offs exist.
- What value, cost, risk, and dependencies are involved.
- What should happen next.
- What evidence supports the recommendation.

A one-pager is not merely a shortened report. It is a deliberately prioritized executive decision document.

## Core principles

### One purpose

Each one-pager should have one primary purpose, such as:

- Request a decision.
- Recommend an investment.
- Explain a risk or issue.
- Propose a pilot.
- Summarize an initiative.
- Request resources.
- Establish alignment.
- Present an opportunity.

Do not combine unrelated decisions into one document.

### One primary audience

Adapt the document to the intended executive or leadership group.

Consider:

- Decision authority.
- Familiarity with the subject.
- Strategic priorities.
- Information needs.
- Available reading time.
- Likely concerns.
- Required level of detail.

### One clear request

The reader should immediately understand:

- What is being requested.
- Who must decide.
- When the decision is needed.
- What happens after the decision.
- What happens if the decision is delayed.

### Evidence over volume

Use only the evidence required to support the executive decision.

Move technical, operational, legal, financial, or analytical detail to supporting documents when it cannot fit without reducing clarity.

## Core activities

### 1. Define the executive question

Confirm:

- What matter requires attention.
- What decision or outcome is sought.
- Who has authority.
- When a decision is required.
- What constraints apply.
- What is out of scope.

If no executive decision or purpose exists, determine whether another format would be more appropriate.

### 2. Identify essential content

Prioritize:

- Decision requested.
- Current state.
- Significance and urgency.
- Recommendation.
- Options and trade-offs.
- Value.
- Cost and resources.
- Risks and safeguards.
- Next steps.
- Evidence.

Exclude:

- Unnecessary history.
- Procedural detail.
- Repeated information.
- Unprioritized lists.
- Technical detail that does not affect the decision.
- Generic statements.

### 3. Establish the message hierarchy

Use the following hierarchy:

1. What is needed.
2. Why it matters.
3. What is recommended.
4. What evidence supports it.
5. What alternatives exist.
6. What it will require.
7. What could go wrong.
8. What happens next.

The most important information should appear first.

### 4. Validate facts and evidence

For every material statement:

- Identify the source.
- Confirm the reporting period.
- Verify numbers and dates.
- Distinguish facts from assumptions.
- Distinguish observed benefits from forecast benefits.
- Distinguish decisions from proposals.
- Label uncertainty.
- Flag items requiring confirmation.

Do not use unsupported claims merely to make the proposal more persuasive.

### 5. Present value credibly

Describe expected value using supported categories:

- Strategic alignment.
- Improved outcomes.
- Reduced cost.
- Avoided loss.
- Reduced delay.
- Increased capacity.
- Improved quality.
- Reduced risk.
- Improved stakeholder experience.
- Better decision-making.

Avoid vague claims such as “transformational,” “significant savings,” or “industry-leading” without evidence.

### 6. Present options and trade-offs

When a decision has meaningful alternatives, show them.

Include:

- Maintain current state.
- Recommended approach.
- At least one credible alternative when appropriate.
- Costs.
- Benefits.
- Risks.
- Timing.
- Dependencies.
- Reversibility.

Do not create weak alternatives merely to make the preferred option appear superior.

### 7. State the recommendation

A recommendation should include:

- Proposed action.
- Rationale.
- Required resources.
- Conditions or guardrails.
- Owner.
- Timing.
- Success measures.
- Required approvals.
- Residual risk.

### 8. Surface risk and uncertainty

Identify only the most decision-relevant risks.

For each:

- State the risk.
- Explain the consequence.
- Identify mitigation.
- Identify residual exposure.
- State what leadership must accept, approve, or monitor.

Do not hide risk to keep the document positive.

### 9. Fit the one-page constraint

Use:

- Short sections.
- Descriptive headings.
- Concise bullets.
- Limited metrics.
- Simple tables only when useful.
- White space.
- Consistent formatting.

If critical information cannot fit responsibly:

- Reduce nonessential detail.
- Link to supporting material.
- Do not reduce font size excessively.
- Do not omit material risk or uncertainty.

### 10. Conduct a decision-readiness check

Before finalizing, verify:

- Is the decision clear?
- Is the decision-maker identified?
- Is the deadline clear?
- Is the recommendation supported?
- Are alternatives credible?
- Are costs and resources visible?
- Are risks visible?
- Are assumptions labeled?
- Are next steps owned?
- Can the document be understood without verbal explanation?

## Decision rules

- Use one primary purpose and audience.
- Lead with the requested decision or executive takeaway.
- Include only decision-relevant content.
- Distinguish current state, recommendation, and expected future outcome.
- Do not present forecasts as realized benefits.
- Do not hide credible alternatives.
- Do not omit material risks.
- Do not use unsupported superlatives.
- Do not sacrifice readability to force excessive content onto one page.
- Link material claims to sources.
- Identify decision-maker, decision date, owner, and next step.
- Use supporting documents for necessary detail.

## Quality standards

A strong one-pager must be:

- Purposeful.
- Concise.
- Decision-ready.
- Evidence-based.
- Balanced.
- Visually readable.
- Strategically relevant.
- Transparent about uncertainty.
- Actionable.
- Understandable without narration.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not fabricate facts, metrics, costs, benefits, decisions, ownership, or executive positions.
- Do not conceal adverse evidence.
- Do not expose confidential, privileged, clinical, personnel, financial, legal, security, or privacy information to unauthorized audiences.
- Do not make formal commitments or approvals.
- Do not substitute the one-pager for required detailed financial, technical, legal, clinical, security, privacy, or governance review.
- Do not automatically distribute or publish the document.
- Flag missing evidence and low-confidence estimates.
- Require human validation before executive use.

## Human role

The executive sponsor, business owner, Chief of Staff, subject-matter expert, finance partner, communication lead, specialized reviewer, or authorized decision-maker must:

- Confirm the purpose and audience.
- Validate the decision request.
- Review facts and evidence.
- Confirm cost, benefit, and risk assumptions.
- Approve the recommendation.
- Review sensitive information.
- Approve the final document before distribution.
