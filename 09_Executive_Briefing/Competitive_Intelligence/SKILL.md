# Skill: Competitive Intelligence

## Purpose

This skill collects, evaluates, synthesizes, and compares approved information about competitors, peer institutions, vendors, partners, markets, technologies, and alternative strategic approaches.

It helps leadership understand:

- What relevant organizations are doing.
- How their strategies, capabilities, investments, and outcomes compare.
- What market, technology, regulatory, and stakeholder trends are emerging.
- Where opportunities, threats, and capability gaps may exist.
- Which developments are material to an organizational decision.
- What evidence supports each conclusion.
- What remains uncertain or requires further research.

The skill provides ethical, lawful, and source-linked decision support. It does not conduct covert intelligence, obtain restricted information, access unauthorized systems, impersonate individuals, or present speculation as fact.

## Core activities

### 1. Define the intelligence question

Start with the decision to be supported.

Clarify:

- What leadership needs to understand.
- Which organizations or alternatives are relevant.
- What geographic, market, technology, or institutional scope applies.
- What time horizon matters.
- What comparison criteria will be used.
- What sources are permitted.
- What information is out of scope.
- When the research cutoff occurs.

Avoid collecting broad competitor information without a defined executive purpose.

### 2. Develop the research plan

Identify:

- Priority questions.
- Target organizations.
- Required primary sources.
- Useful secondary sources.
- Required comparison dimensions.
- Known evidence gaps.
- Relevant internal context.
- Required specialized review.

Prioritize authoritative sources that directly support the intelligence question.

### 3. Collect information ethically

Use only authorized sources and methods.

Permitted activities may include:

- Reviewing publicly available materials.
- Reviewing licensed sources available to the organization.
- Using approved internal information.
- Conducting authorized benchmarking or interviews.
- Summarizing official announcements and reports.

Prohibited activities include:

- Misrepresentation or impersonation.
- Circumventing access restrictions.
- Seeking confidential or proprietary information.
- Using stolen, leaked, or improperly obtained data.
- Scraping or automated collection in violation of applicable terms or policy.
- Requesting employees or partners to violate confidentiality.
- Combining personal information in ways that create inappropriate surveillance.

### 4. Evaluate source quality

For each material source, assess:

- Authority.
- Recency.
- Independence.
- Directness.
- Verifiability.
- Potential bias.
- Measurement consistency.
- Licensing or use limitations.

Prefer primary sources for factual claims. Use secondary sources to provide context or independent interpretation.

### 5. Separate facts, claims, and inferences

Classify information as:

- **Verified fact:** directly supported by a reliable source.
- **Organization-reported claim:** stated by the organization but not independently validated.
- **Third-party assessment:** interpretation from an external source.
- **Inference:** analytical conclusion based on available evidence.
- **Unknown:** information not available or not sufficiently reliable.

Do not convert marketing language into objective performance findings.

### 6. Build comparable profiles

For each organization, organize information consistently:

- Strategy.
- Offerings and capabilities.
- Target audiences.
- Technology.
- Partnerships.
- Investments.
- Implementation maturity.
- Governance and controls.
- Reported outcomes.
- Recent developments.
- Strengths and limitations.

Record where data is missing or definitions differ.

### 7. Conduct comparative analysis

Compare organizations using decision-relevant criteria.

The analysis should:

- Use consistent definitions.
- Align measurement periods where possible.
- Explain non-comparable information.
- Avoid ranking organizations based on incomplete data.
- Distinguish scale from effectiveness.
- Distinguish announced plans from implemented capabilities.
- Distinguish reported outcomes from independently validated results.

### 8. Identify strategic signals

Look for corroborated patterns involving:

- Investment.
- Product or service launches.
- Partnerships.
- Hiring and capability development.
- Technology adoption.
- Regulatory response.
- Market or stakeholder behavior.
- Changes in operating models.
- Shifts in public priorities.

A single announcement should generally be treated as a signal, not proof of a durable strategic shift.

### 9. Evaluate organizational implications

Connect external evidence to internal context:

- Strategic priorities.
- Existing capabilities.
- Constraints.
- Risk tolerance.
- Operating model.
- Stakeholder needs.
- Current investments.
- Available resources.
- Governance requirements.

Avoid recommending imitation solely because another organization adopted an approach. Evaluate organizational fit.

### 10. Develop response options

Possible responses may include:

- Continue current strategy.
- Accelerate.
- Differentiate.
- Partner.
- Build.
- Buy.
- Pilot.
- Monitor.
- Delay.
- Stop.

For each option, explain:

- Evidence.
- Benefits.
- Risks.
- Dependencies.
- Required capabilities.
- Decision criteria.
- Uncertainty.

### 11. Establish ongoing monitoring

Recommend:

- Organizations and topics to monitor.
- Sources.
- Cadence.
- Trigger events.
- Owners.
- Conditions requiring updated analysis.

Clearly distinguish a one-time intelligence brief from continuous monitoring.

## Decision rules

- Begin with a defined leadership question.
- Use authoritative and recent sources.
- Include publication and access dates.
- Distinguish official claims from independently validated facts.
- Label inference explicitly.
- Do not rank organizations using incomparable data.
- Do not treat announced intent as implemented capability.
- Do not assume public silence means inactivity.
- Do not infer confidential strategy from weak signals.
- Avoid false precision where evidence is limited.
- Consider partnership and learning opportunities, not only competitive threats.
- Connect external evidence to internal organizational fit.
- Preserve contradictory evidence rather than hiding it.
- Use a clear research cutoff date because competitive information changes.

## Quality standards

A strong competitive-intelligence output must be:

- Decision-focused.
- Ethical and lawful.
- Source-linked.
- Current as of a stated date.
- Comparable.
- Transparent.
- Confidence-aware.
- Balanced.
- Strategically relevant.
- Actionable.

Leadership should be able to distinguish what is known, what was claimed, what was inferred, and what remains unknown.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not obtain or use confidential, stolen, leaked, restricted, or improperly acquired information.
- Do not bypass authentication, paywalls, robots restrictions, access controls, or contractual limitations.
- Do not impersonate another person or misrepresent the purpose of research.
- Do not collect unnecessary personal information.
- Do not make unsupported accusations or speculate about motives.
- Do not reproduce copyrighted materials beyond permitted use.
- Do not present marketing claims as verified performance.
- Do not provide definitive legal, financial, regulatory, security, privacy, or clinical conclusions.
- Do not contact competitors, peers, vendors, or partners automatically.
- Flag stale, conflicting, incomplete, or low-confidence information.
- Require human review before consequential strategic use or external distribution.

## Human role

The executive sponsor, strategy lead, research owner, legal or compliance partner, procurement partner, or specialized reviewer must:

- Confirm the intelligence question and scope.
- Approve sources and research methods.
- Validate internal strategic context.
- Review sensitive comparisons.
- Confirm legal, contractual, and ethical requirements.
- Evaluate strategic options.
- Approve any outreach or external use.
- Determine ongoing monitoring priorities.
