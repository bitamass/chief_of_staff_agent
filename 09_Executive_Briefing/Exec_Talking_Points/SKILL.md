# Skill: Executive Talking Points

## Purpose

This skill prepares concise, natural, evidence-based talking points for an executive’s specific audience, engagement, and desired outcome.

It helps the executive understand:

- What the audience most needs to hear.
- What the executive wants the audience to understand, decide, feel, or do.
- Which messages should receive the greatest emphasis.
- Which facts and examples support those messages.
- How to address likely questions and concerns.
- What commitments the executive may or may not make.
- Which topics require careful handling or referral.

The skill produces speaking guidance rather than a full speech unless a full script is explicitly requested.

## Core principles

### Spoken, not written

Talking points should sound natural when spoken.

Use:

- Short sentences.
- Clear transitions.
- Direct language.
- Memorable message headlines.
- Limited jargon.
- Evidence that can be understood quickly.

Avoid:

- Dense paragraphs.
- Excessive detail.
- Long lists of statistics.
- Formal report language.
- Claims that are difficult to explain verbally.

### Audience-specific

The same subject may require different talking points for:

- Executives.
- Employees.
- Board members.
- Clinicians.
- Technical teams.
- Faculty.
- Students.
- Partners.
- Vendors.
- Regulators.
- Community members.
- Media.

Adapt detail, tone, framing, examples, and calls to action to the audience.

### Outcome-oriented

Every set of talking points should support a defined outcome, such as:

- Informing.
- Building understanding.
- Securing alignment.
- Requesting a decision.
- Motivating action.
- Addressing concern.
- Reinforcing accountability.
- Establishing confidence.
- Communicating change.

Do not merely summarize the topic.

## Core activities

### 1. Define the engagement

Confirm:

- Who is speaking.
- To whom.
- When and where.
- For how long.
- In what format.
- Before or after which other speakers.
- With what desired outcome.
- Under what communication constraints.

### 2. Establish the message hierarchy

Create:

- One primary message.
- Two to four supporting messages.
- Evidence for each supporting message.
- One clear desired action or conclusion.

Avoid giving every fact equal weight. The audience should be able to remember the primary message after the engagement.

### 3. Connect messages to audience needs

For every main point:

- Explain why it matters to the audience.
- Address the audience’s likely interests.
- Anticipate how the audience may interpret the message.
- Recognize legitimate concerns.
- Avoid assuming shared background knowledge.

### 4. Validate facts and positions

For each material statement:

- Identify the source.
- Confirm the reporting period.
- Distinguish approved decisions from proposals.
- Distinguish observed outcomes from expected outcomes.
- Distinguish organizational positions from analytical recommendations.
- Flag claims requiring executive or specialized confirmation.

Do not place unsupported statements into the executive’s voice.

### 5. Reflect the executive’s voice

Use prior approved communications and explicit preferences to reflect:

- Tone.
- Vocabulary.
- Leadership themes.
- Degree of formality.
- Preferred framing.
- Communication style.

Do not fabricate personal stories, emotional reactions, quotations, beliefs, or experiences.

### 6. Prepare for questions

Anticipate:

- Clarifying questions.
- Requests for evidence.
- Concerns about impact.
- Questions about timing.
- Questions about cost or resources.
- Questions about accountability.
- Questions about risk.
- Questions regarding unapproved or confidential matters.
- Challenges to the stated position.

For each question:

- Provide a concise approved answer.
- Identify supporting evidence.
- Provide a bridge to the main message.
- Flag when the executive should defer or refer.

### 7. Address sensitive subjects

When a topic is sensitive:

- Acknowledge legitimate concern.
- Use verified facts.
- Avoid speculation.
- Avoid defensive or dismissive language.
- State what is known, unknown, decided, and still under review.
- Do not disclose restricted information.
- Identify the appropriate follow-up process.

### 8. Structure the delivery

A typical structure is:

1. Acknowledge the audience.
2. State the purpose.
3. Deliver the primary message.
4. Present supporting points.
5. Address decisions, implications, or concerns.
6. State the call to action.
7. Close with a forward-looking message.

Adapt the structure to the engagement.

### 9. Calibrate to available time

Estimate speaking duration.

As a general planning assumption, conversational executive remarks may average approximately 120–150 words per minute, but actual pace varies.

Provide:

- Essential points.
- Optional expansion points.
- Points to remove if time is shortened.
- Questions or topics that require separate follow-up.

Do not force excessive content into a limited speaking window.

## Decision rules

- Begin with one clear primary message.
- Limit supporting messages to what the audience can reasonably retain.
- Write for spoken delivery.
- Use only approved facts and positions.
- Distinguish decisions from proposals.
- Do not invent executive beliefs, quotations, stories, or commitments.
- Connect every major point to the audience and desired outcome.
- Avoid jargon unless the audience is expected to understand it.
- Include source references outside the spoken text.
- Do not overload remarks with statistics.
- Provide a clear call to action when action is intended.
- Flag sensitive matters and disclosure limitations.
- Preserve uncertainty where facts are incomplete.

## Quality standards

Strong talking points must be:

- Clear.
- Concise.
- Memorable.
- Audience-specific.
- Outcome-oriented.
- Factually supported.
- Natural when spoken.
- Consistent with the executive’s approved position.
- Sensitive to context.
- Easy to shorten or expand.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not fabricate quotations, personal stories, positions, commitments, or emotional reactions.
- Do not include confidential, privileged, personnel, legal, clinical, security, privacy, or restricted information without authorization.
- Do not provide unauthorized public, media, policy, regulatory, or institutional statements.
- Do not hide material uncertainty.
- Do not make unsupported promises.
- Do not automatically publish, distribute, or deliver the talking points.
- Flag claims requiring verification.
- Require human and communication review for consequential engagements.

## Human role

The executive, Chief of Staff, communication lead, subject-matter expert, legal or policy partner, or designated approver must:

- Confirm the audience and desired outcome.
- Approve the primary message.
- Validate factual claims.
- Confirm the executive’s position and voice.
- Approve sensitive responses.
- Determine which commitments may be made.
- Rehearse and adapt the points for delivery.
- Approve the final version before use.
