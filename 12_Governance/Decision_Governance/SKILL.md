# Skill: Decision Governance

## Purpose

This skill establishes a structured, traceable process for preparing, reviewing, making, recording, implementing, and monitoring consequential organizational decisions.

It helps leadership understand:

- What decision is required.
- Who has authority to make it.
- Which consultations and reviews are required.
- What options and evidence should be considered.
- What risks, trade-offs, and stakeholder effects matter.
- Whether the decision package is ready.
- What conditions apply.
- Who owns implementation.
- How outcomes and continued validity will be monitored.

The skill supports responsible decision-making. It does not make, approve, reject, authorize, or implement decisions automatically.

## Core principles

### Clear decision rights

Every consequential decision should identify:

- Decision requester.
- Decision owner.
- Preparer.
- Consultees.
- Reviewers.
- Recommending authority.
- Approving authority.
- Implementation authority.
- Risk-acceptance authority.
- Escalation body.

### Evidence-based deliberation

Decision-makers should receive:

- Defined problem or opportunity.
- Credible options.
- Evaluation criteria.
- Benefits.
- Costs.
- Risks.
- Dependencies.
- Stakeholder implications.
- Assumptions.
- Evidence limitations.
- Recommendation.

### Traceability

The decision record should preserve:

- Authority.
- Materials considered.
- Options.
- Rationale.
- Conditions.
- Implementation ownership.
- Outcomes.
- Subsequent modifications.

### Proportionality

The governance process should reflect:

- Materiality.
- Risk.
- Reversibility.
- Cost.
- Scope.
- Stakeholder impact.
- Regulatory or policy obligations.
- Potential precedent.

Not every routine operational decision requires executive governance.

## Core activities

### 1. Define the decision

Create a specific decision statement:

> Decide whether to [action] for [scope] by [date] in order to [outcome].

Confirm:

- Scope.
- Decision owner.
- Required date.
- Intended outcome.
- Consequence of delay.
- Items outside the decision.

Avoid vague requests such as “review,” “discuss,” or “provide thoughts” when a decision is required.

### 2. Classify the decision

Determine:

- Decision type.
- Organizational level.
- Materiality.
- Reversibility.
- Risk.
- Affected stakeholders.
- Financial and resource thresholds.
- Required governance path.
- Potential precedent.

Classification supports routing but does not replace authoritative human interpretation.

### 3. Validate decision authority

Use current approved:

- Delegations of authority.
- Governance charters.
- Policies.
- Contracts.
- Regulatory requirements.
- Board or executive decisions.

Distinguish:

- Advice.
- Review.
- Recommendation.
- Approval.
- Implementation authority.
- Risk acceptance.

Do not infer authority from job title or seniority alone.

### 4. Identify required participants

Identify:

- Decision-maker.
- Request owner.
- Business and operational owners.
- Subject-matter experts.
- Stakeholders affected.
- Specialized reviewers.
- Governance bodies.
- Implementation owners.
- Informed parties.

Avoid inviting participants who have no material decision, consultation, or information role.

### 5. Define evaluation criteria

Establish criteria before comparing options when possible.

Criteria may include:

- Strategic alignment.
- Expected value.
- Cost.
- Feasibility.
- Risk.
- Time.
- Stakeholder effect.
- Compliance.
- Reversibility.
- Evidence quality.
- Readiness.

Use approved weights when available. Do not manipulate criteria to predetermine the result.

### 6. Develop credible options

Include:

- Recommended option.
- Credible alternatives.
- Current-state or no-action option when relevant.
- Phased or pilot option when appropriate.
- Deferral when additional evidence is needed.

Do not create artificially weak alternatives.

### 7. Evaluate options

For each option:

- Describe benefits.
- Describe costs.
- Identify resources.
- Assess feasibility.
- Identify risks.
- Identify dependencies.
- Evaluate stakeholder impact.
- Assess timing and reversibility.
- State assumptions.
- Assess evidence confidence.

Distinguish facts, forecasts, owner assessments, and analytical recommendations.

### 8. Assess decision readiness

A decision may be ready when:

- Authority is confirmed.
- Scope is clear.
- Options are credible.
- Evidence is sufficient.
- Required reviews are complete.
- Risks and trade-offs are visible.
- Implementation ownership exists.
- Conditions and monitoring are defined.

A decision should not be delayed solely because perfect information is unavailable. Identify whether uncertainty is acceptable, manageable through conditions, or too material to proceed.

### 9. Support deliberation

Prepare concise materials that identify:

- Decision.
- Context.
- Options.
- Recommendation.
- Trade-offs.
- Questions.
- Risks.
- Assumptions.
- Dissenting evidence.
- Requested deadline.

Do not suppress credible contrary evidence.

### 10. Record the decision

Capture:

- Exact decision.
- Decision-maker.
- Date.
- Authority.
- Options considered.
- Rationale.
- Conditions.
- Effective date.
- Implementation owner.
- Review and expiration dates.
- Source.

Do not infer a decision from discussion, silence, or meeting attendance.

### 11. Govern implementation

Identify:

- Actions.
- Owners.
- Resources.
- Milestones.
- Conditions.
- Reporting.
- Monitoring.
- Escalation.
- Success measures.
- Closure criteria.

A decision without implementation accountability is incomplete.

### 12. Monitor outcomes and validity

Review:

- Implementation progress.
- Conditions.
- Outcomes.
- Benefits.
- Risks.
- Unintended effects.
- Material changes.
- Continued relevance.
- Need for renewal, modification, suspension, reversal, or closure.

Do not assume a decision remains valid when its conditions or context materially change.

## Decision rules

- Require a clear decision statement.
- Confirm authority.
- Distinguish advice, recommendation, approval, and authorization.
- Use credible alternatives.
- Apply consistent criteria.
- Preserve dissenting or conflicting evidence.
- Do not present forecasts as realized outcomes.
- Do not infer approval.
- Preserve conditions and scope.
- Assign implementation and monitoring ownership.
- Define review and expiration when appropriate.
- Trigger re-review after material change.
- Separate analytical recommendation from the authorized decision.

## Quality standards

Strong decision governance must be:

- Authority-based.
- Proportionate.
- Evidence-based.
- Option-aware.
- Risk-aware.
- Transparent.
- Traceable.
- Condition-aware.
- Implementation-linked.
- Outcome-monitored.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not make, approve, reject, authorize, or implement decisions.
- Do not fabricate authority, evidence, options, rationale, votes, conditions, or outcomes.
- Do not provide definitive legal, regulatory, clinical, security, privacy, financial, procurement, labor, research, or policy determinations.
- Do not bypass required governance or specialized review.
- Do not expose confidential, privileged, personnel, clinical, research, legal, security, privacy, financial, or procurement information to unauthorized audiences.
- Flag missing, conflicting, stale, or low-confidence evidence.
- Require authorized human review for consequential use.

## Human role

The decision requester, decision owner, authorized decision-maker, governance body, business and operational owners, specialized reviewers, implementation owner, risk owner, and executive sponsor must:

- Confirm decision scope.
- Validate authority.
- Approve evaluation criteria.
- Review evidence and options.
- Make and record the decision.
- Establish conditions.
- Authorize implementation.
- Monitor outcomes.
- Modify, renew, suspend, reverse, or close the decision when appropriate.
