# Skill: Issue Escalation

## Purpose

This skill evaluates active issues and recommends a proportionate, traceable escalation pathway when resolution requires additional authority, resources, coordination, governance, or specialized response.

It helps leadership understand:

- What has occurred.
- What is currently affected.
- How severe and urgent the issue is.
- Whether containment is effective.
- Who owns resolution.
- Why the current level cannot resolve it.
- What specific decision, resource, or intervention is required.
- Who has authority to provide it.
- When a response is required.
- Whether a specialized incident or notification process applies.

The skill recommends escalation for human authorization. It does not automatically escalate, notify, declare an incident, contact external parties, assign blame, or make formal legal, clinical, regulatory, security, privacy, or safety determinations.

## Core distinctions

### Issue

A current condition or event negatively affecting an objective, service, obligation, stakeholder, or outcome.

### Risk

An uncertain future event or condition.

### Blocker

A current condition preventing required work or a decision from proceeding.

### Incident

An event handled through a defined specialized response process, such as cybersecurity, privacy, clinical safety, service continuity, workplace safety, or crisis management.

### Escalation

Transfer of an unresolved matter to a person or body with additional authority, resources, responsibility, or specialized expertise.

### Notification

Communication required by policy, law, regulation, contract, procedure, or governance. Notification and escalation may overlap but are not identical.

## Core activities

### 1. Validate the issue

Confirm:

- The condition exists now.
- The issue is described clearly.
- Evidence exists.
- Affected scope is identified.
- Owner is identified.
- Current status is known.
- The matter is not better classified as a risk, blocker, dependency, action, or assumption.
- A specialized incident process is not already required.

Do not escalate unverified claims as established facts.

### 2. Normalize the issue statement

Use a clear structure:

> [Condition] has occurred, affecting [objective, service, stakeholder, or obligation], resulting in [current impact] and requiring [resolution] by [date].

Separate:

- Confirmed facts.
- Unconfirmed information.
- Assumptions.
- Potential future consequences.

### 3. Assess severity and urgency

Evaluate severity based on consequence:

- Strategic.
- Operational.
- Financial.
- Clinical or safety.
- Research.
- Technology.
- Security and privacy.
- Legal, regulatory, or compliance.
- Workforce.
- Stakeholder and reputation.
- Continuity.

Evaluate urgency based on:

- Time before harm increases.
- Deadline.
- Rate of deterioration.
- Reversibility.
- External obligation.
- Available response window.

Severity and urgency must remain distinct.

### 4. Assess containment

Determine:

- What immediate action occurred.
- Whether impact is stabilized.
- What remains exposed.
- Whether the workaround is sustainable.
- Whether containment creates new risk.
- Whether specialized verification is required.

Containment is not the same as resolution.

### 5. Identify resolution ownership

Distinguish:

- Person reporting the issue.
- Issue owner.
- Resolution owner.
- Operational owner.
- Decision-maker.
- Resource authority.
- Risk-acceptance authority.
- Communication authority.
- Specialized-response owner.

Do not assign accountability solely to the person who discovered the issue.

### 6. Review resolution attempts

Determine:

- Actions already taken.
- Decisions requested.
- Resources requested.
- Follow-up history.
- Responses.
- Revised dates.
- Prior escalations.
- Why the issue remains unresolved.

Escalation should not duplicate an action that has not yet been given a reasonable opportunity to succeed unless urgency requires it.

### 7. Select the lowest effective escalation level

Use the lowest level that possesses the authority, resources, or expertise required to resolve the issue.

Possible escalation paths include:

- Team owner.
- Program or operational lead.
- Sponsor.
- Cross-functional leadership.
- Governance body.
- Executive.
- Specialized security, privacy, clinical, legal, regulatory, safety, continuity, HR, or crisis process.

Do not escalate directly to senior leadership when an authorized lower-level owner can resolve the matter, unless severity or urgency requires it.

### 8. Define the escalation request

Every escalation should specify:

- What happened.
- What is affected.
- Current containment.
- What was attempted.
- Why current authority is insufficient.
- What decision, resource, intervention, or expertise is required.
- Who must respond.
- Required response date.
- Consequence of delay.
- Next escalation trigger.

An escalation without a specific request is only a status notification.

### 9. Apply specialized routing

If the issue may involve:

- Safety.
- Clinical harm.
- Cybersecurity.
- Privacy breach.
- Legal or regulatory obligation.
- Research-participant protection.
- Workplace safety or labor.
- Service continuity.
- Fraud or financial misconduct.
- Crisis communications.

recommend immediate use of the relevant authorized process. Do not attempt to replace or reinterpret that process.

### 10. Manage communications

Identify:

- Who needs to know.
- Why.
- When.
- What information can be shared.
- Who is authorized to communicate.
- What approval is required.
- What documentation must be retained.

Avoid unnecessary disclosure of sensitive information.

### 11. Track escalation lifecycle

Track:

- Issue identified.
- Validated.
- Assigned.
- Contained.
- Escalated.
- Decision or resource provided.
- Resolution implemented.
- Effectiveness validated.
- Closed.
- Reopened.

Preserve dates, owners, decisions, and evidence.

### 12. Validate resolution and closure

An issue may be closed when:

- Resolution is implemented.
- Effectiveness is verified.
- Required approvals exist.
- Residual risk is documented.
- Corrective and preventive actions are assigned.
- Monitoring is established.
- Authorized owner confirms closure.

Do not close an issue solely because the immediate symptom disappeared.

### 13. Identify systemic patterns

Across issues, detect:

- Recurring causes.
- Repeated control failures.
- Escalation bottlenecks.
- Chronic ownership gaps.
- Recurring vendor or dependency failures.
- Issues repeatedly reopened.
- Delayed decisions.
- Ineffective containment.
- Missing preventive action.

## Decision rules

- Validate the issue before escalating.
- Distinguish issue, risk, blocker, and incident.
- Assess severity and urgency separately.
- Confirm current containment.
- Identify the resolution authority required.
- Use the lowest effective escalation level.
- State a specific requested action.
- Include a response deadline.
- Do not infer blame, negligence, or misconduct.
- Do not bypass specialized response processes.
- Minimize sensitive-information disclosure.
- Preserve escalation history.
- Do not close without evidence.
- Separate recommendations from authorized escalation decisions.

## Quality standards

A strong issue-escalation output must be:

- Accurate.
- Timely.
- Proportionate.
- Authority-aware.
- Resolution-oriented.
- Evidence-based.
- Confidentiality-aware.
- Traceable.
- Specialized-process aware.
- Closure-validated.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not automatically escalate or notify anyone.
- Do not declare an incident, emergency, breach, violation, or legal obligation.
- Do not make personnel, performance, disciplinary, or motive judgments.
- Do not provide definitive legal, clinical, security, privacy, financial, regulatory, labor, or safety determinations.
- Do not contact external parties or authorities.
- Do not expose confidential, privileged, clinical, research, personnel, legal, security, privacy, financial, or procurement information to unauthorized audiences.
- Do not bypass required governance or response processes.
- Flag missing, conflicting, stale, or low-confidence information.
- Require authorized human and specialized review.

## Human role

The issue owner, resolution owner, operational leader, sponsor, governance body, specialized response owner, legal or policy partner, communication authority, risk owner, and authorized executive must:

- Validate the issue.
- Confirm severity and urgency.
- Approve escalation.
- Select recipients.
- Authorize communications.
- Provide decisions and resources.
- Confirm resolution.
- Approve residual risk and closure.
- Ensure corrective and preventive actions are completed.
