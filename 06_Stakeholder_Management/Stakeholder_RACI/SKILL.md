# Skill: Stakeholder RACI

## Purpose

This skill creates and evaluates a RACI matrix that clarifies responsibility and accountability for an initiative, deliverable, decision, process, or organizational change.

It helps leadership determine:

- Who performs the work.
- Who owns the final outcome.
- Who must provide input.
- Who must receive information.
- Where responsibilities are missing, duplicated, inconsistent, or unclear.
- Whether assigned roles possess the authority, expertise, and capacity needed.
- Which responsibility conflicts require leadership resolution.

The skill supports coordination and governance; it does not replace formal delegation of authority, job responsibilities, contracts, policy, or human approval.

## RACI definitions

### Responsible

The person or role that performs, coordinates, or directly manages the work.

- A row may have more than one Responsible role when the work genuinely requires shared execution.
- Responsibility should be specific enough to avoid ambiguity.
- The Responsible role must understand the expected outcome and deadline.

### Accountable

The single person or role that owns the outcome and has authority to approve, accept, or answer for the result.

- Each material row should normally have exactly one Accountable role.
- Accountability cannot be assigned solely because someone has the highest title.
- The Accountable role must have sufficient authority over the decision, resources, or outcome.
- Committees may be accountable only when formal governance establishes collective accountability and the decision process is documented.

### Consulted

A person or role whose expertise or input is required before the work is completed or a decision is made.

- Consultation is two-way.
- Consulted roles should be limited to participants whose input materially affects quality, compliance, feasibility, risk, or acceptance.
- A stakeholder must not be marked Consulted merely as a courtesy.

### Informed

A person or role who must receive relevant information but does not provide required approval or input.

- Communication is primarily one-way.
- The timing and type of information should be appropriate to the stakeholder’s role.
- A stakeholder who has approval authority must not be classified only as Informed.

## Core activities

### 1. Define the scope

- Identify the initiative, process, decision, or deliverable covered by the RACI.
- Establish the appropriate level of detail.
- Define the period, phase, or lifecycle stage covered.
- Identify authoritative documents and governance structures.
- Record items that are explicitly out of scope.

### 2. Identify work requiring ownership

Break the scope into meaningful:

- Deliverables.
- Decisions.
- Milestones.
- Reviews and approvals.
- Operational activities.
- Handoffs.
- Risk and issue responses.
- Communication and reporting activities.
- Governance and assurance responsibilities.

Avoid creating RACI rows for tasks that are too small to require meaningful responsibility clarification.

### 3. Identify relevant roles

- Use roles rather than individual names when responsibility should persist through staffing changes.
- Use individual names when the responsibility is specific, temporary, or formally assigned to that individual.
- Include governing, approving, funding, operational, technical, clinical, security, privacy, legal, finance, HR, procurement, records-management, and stakeholder-engagement roles when relevant.
- Exclude participants who have no material responsibility or information need.

### 4. Propose assignments from evidence

For every proposed assignment:

- Identify the supporting charter, policy, decision, meeting record, job responsibility, or other evidence.
- Confirm that the role has relevant authority or expertise.
- Distinguish documented assignments from recommendations.
- Label uncertain assignments for validation.
- Do not infer accountability from meeting attendance, seniority, or general involvement.

### 5. Validate RACI integrity

Check each row for:

- At least one Responsible role.
- Exactly one Accountable role unless formal collective accountability applies.
- Appropriate Consulted participants.
- Necessary Informed participants.
- No unsupported assignments.
- No missing approval or assurance functions.
- Alignment between responsibility and authority.
- Reasonable workload and concentration.

### 6. Detect conflicts and gaps

Identify:

- Missing ownership.
- Multiple accountable parties.
- Overlapping execution responsibilities.
- Excessive consultation.
- Inadequate consultation.
- Accountability without decision authority.
- Responsibility without resources or expertise.
- Governance roles classified incorrectly.
- Critical dependencies without handoff ownership.
- Individuals or teams with unsustainable assignment loads.

### 7. Recommend resolution

For every material issue:

- State the problem.
- Explain its potential effect.
- Identify the roles involved.
- Recommend an assignment or validation question.
- Identify who must approve the correction.
- Set an escalation path if agreement cannot be reached.

## Decision rules

- Every material deliverable or decision should have at least one Responsible role.
- Every material deliverable or decision should normally have exactly one Accountable role.
- The Accountable role must possess relevant authority; seniority alone is insufficient.
- Avoid assigning entire departments when a specific role can be identified.
- Avoid assigning an executive as Accountable for operational work that belongs to a designated owner.
- Avoid excessive Consulted assignments that make timely decisions impractical.
- Do not classify required reviewers or approvers only as Informed.
- Do not create artificial shared accountability to avoid resolving ownership.
- Separate responsibility for producing a deliverable from authority to approve it.
- Separate governance oversight from operational execution.
- Distinguish current assignments from recommended future assignments.

## Quality standards

A strong RACI must be:

- Complete: material work and decisions are represented.
- Specific: rows describe clear outcomes rather than vague responsibilities.
- Singular in accountability: ownership is unambiguous.
- Evidence-based: assignments connect to approved information.
- Role-appropriate: authority and expertise match the assignment.
- Balanced: responsibilities are reasonably distributed.
- Governed: formal approval and assurance roles are respected.
- Maintainable: assignments can be reviewed as the work changes.
- Actionable: conflicts and gaps have recommended resolutions.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not invent authority, responsibility, expertise, or delegation.
- Do not change formal organizational accountability through the generated matrix.
- Do not assign work automatically or notify stakeholders.
- Do not treat the output as an approved governance document until validated.
- Do not infer responsibility from job title alone.
- Do not include confidential personnel judgments or performance assessments.
- Clearly mark proposed assignments, unresolved conflicts, and missing evidence.
- Require review when legal, regulatory, clinical, security, privacy, financial, HR, labor, or procurement responsibilities are involved.

## Human role

The executive sponsor, program owner, governance lead, or designated authority must:

- Confirm the scope and level of detail.
- Validate each Responsible and Accountable assignment.
- Confirm formal authority and delegation.
- Resolve competing accountability claims.
- Verify capacity and resource availability.
- Approve changes to responsibility.
- Communicate the approved RACI to participants.
- Review the RACI when scope, staffing, governance, or milestones change.
