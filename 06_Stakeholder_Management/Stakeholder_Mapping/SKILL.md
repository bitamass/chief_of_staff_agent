# Skill: Stakeholder Mapping

## Purpose

This skill identifies, classifies, and analyzes the people, groups, organizations, and governance bodies that influence or are affected by an initiative, decision, organizational change, or strategic objective.

It helps leadership understand:

- Who has formal authority.
- Who has practical influence.
- Who is affected by the work.
- Who can enable, delay, redirect, or block progress.
- How stakeholders are connected.
- Where support, opposition, uncertainty, or representation gaps exist.
- Which stakeholders require priority attention.

The output provides the evidence base for engagement planning, communication planning, governance routing, decision support, and change management.

## Core activities

### 1. Define the mapping scope

- Identify the initiative, decision, change, or objective being mapped.
- Define the decisions, milestones, and outcomes that make stakeholder involvement relevant.
- Establish whether the map covers an organization, program, project, decision, or specific implementation phase.
- Identify the timeframe and expected use of the map.

### 2. Identify stakeholders

Identify individuals and groups who:

- Possess formal decision or approval authority.
- Provide funding, governance, oversight, or assurance.
- Own or implement affected operations.
- Supply essential information, technology, resources, or expertise.
- Influence executive or organizational decisions.
- Are affected by the initiative or its outcomes.
- May enable, oppose, delay, or redirect implementation.
- Represent important workforce, clinical, community, customer, partner, or regulatory perspectives.

Do not limit identification to people appearing on an organization chart. Consider formal and informal influence while clearly distinguishing between them.

### 3. Classify stakeholder roles

Assign one or more evidence-based roles:

- Executive sponsor.
- Decision-maker.
- Approver.
- Governance or oversight body.
- Advisor or subject-matter expert.
- Influencer.
- Program or operational owner.
- Implementer.
- Funder or resource owner.
- Risk, legal, security, privacy, or compliance reviewer.
- Partner or vendor.
- Affected individual or community.
- Communication recipient.

Clearly distinguish formal responsibility from perceived influence.

### 4. Assess stakeholder characteristics

Evaluate each stakeholder using available evidence:

- Authority.
- Influence.
- Interest.
- Impact.
- Awareness.
- Current support.
- Desired involvement.
- Ability to enable or obstruct.
- Relationship strength.
- Urgency of engagement.
- Information needs.
- Confidence in the assessment.

Unknown information must be labeled as unknown rather than inferred.

### 5. Map stakeholder relationships

Identify:

- Reporting and governance relationships.
- Advisory and decision pathways.
- Funding and resource dependencies.
- Operational dependencies.
- Informal influence relationships.
- Alliances and shared priorities.
- Conflicts and competing interests.
- Communication pathways.
- Stakeholders who connect different groups.
- Potential bottlenecks or overdependence on a single relationship.

Formal and informal relationships must be presented separately when that distinction matters.

### 6. Identify risks and gaps

Surface:

- Missing decision-makers or approvers.
- Affected groups without representation.
- Stakeholders with unclear roles.
- Conflicting ownership or authority.
- Overlooked governance requirements.
- High-influence stakeholders with low awareness.
- Strong opposition or unresolved concerns.
- Dependence on unverified assumptions.
- Outdated stakeholder information.
- Concentration of influence in a single person or group.

### 7. Prioritize stakeholders

Recommend priority using a combination of:

- Authority.
- Influence.
- Impact.
- Interest.
- Urgency.
- Dependency.
- Risk.
- Required contribution to the desired outcome.

High organizational rank alone must not determine priority.

## Decision rules

- Distinguish formal authority from informal influence.
- Distinguish stakeholder impact from stakeholder interest.
- Do not interpret meeting attendance as influence or support.
- Do not interpret silence as agreement.
- Do not label a stakeholder supportive, neutral, concerned, or opposed without evidence.
- Do not assign approval authority unless documented.
- Do not omit affected groups simply because they lack formal authority.
- Do not treat all stakeholders as requiring the same level of engagement.
- Mark uncertain assessments with an appropriate confidence level.
- Include the date or reporting period because stakeholder relationships may change.

## Quality standards

A strong stakeholder map must be:

- Complete enough to support the defined decision or initiative.
- Focused on stakeholders materially connected to the scope.
- Evidence-based and source-linked.
- Clear about formal authority and informal influence.
- Inclusive of affected and underrepresented groups.
- Transparent about assumptions and missing information.
- Actionable for engagement planning.
- Current and versioned.
- Reviewable by human owners.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not invent stakeholder relationships, opinions, motives, influence, or support.
- Do not use sensitive personal characteristics to score or prioritize stakeholders.
- Do not infer political, medical, demographic, or protected information.
- Do not characterize individuals negatively without documented, relevant evidence.
- Do not expose confidential organizational relationships or restricted information to unauthorized audiences.
- Do not automatically contact stakeholders, assign owners, or make commitments.
- Treat stakeholder assessments as planning aids, not objective judgments about individuals.
- Require human validation before the map informs consequential decisions or communications.

## Human role

The executive sponsor, initiative owner, or stakeholder-management lead must:

- Confirm the scope and intended use.
- Validate the stakeholder inventory.
- Verify authority, influence, and relationship assessments.
- Correct unsupported or outdated assumptions.
- Confirm which information can be shared.
- Approve priority classifications.
- Determine the appropriate engagement strategy.
- Review and update the map as circumstances change.
