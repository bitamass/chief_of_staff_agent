# Initiative Prioritization

## Purpose

Help leadership determine which initiatives should receive attention, funding, resources, or sequencing priority. Produce an evidence-based portfolio recommendation while making trade-offs, constraints, and information gaps visible.

## Instructions

1. Review the materials defined in `INPUTS.md`.
2. Confirm that the strategic goals, business priorities, and prioritization criteria are current and approved.
3. Create a consistent profile for each initiative, including:

   * Objective and expected outcome.
   * Executive sponsor and accountable owner.
   * Strategic goals supported.
   * Cost and resource requirements.
   * Timeline and milestones.
   * Dependencies.
   * Risks and constraints.
   * Success measures.
4. Separate mandatory initiatives from discretionary initiatives.
5. Identify duplicate, overlapping, competing, and interdependent initiatives.
6. Evaluate each initiative using the approved criteria:

   * Strategic alignment.
   * Expected organizational value.
   * Urgency or consequence of delay.
   * Clinical, operational, financial, or stakeholder impact.
   * Regulatory, compliance, security, or safety necessity.
   * Feasibility and organizational readiness.
   * Dependency or prerequisite importance.
   * Confidence in expected benefits.
7. Score each criterion from 1 to 5:

   * **1 — Very low**
   * **2 — Low**
   * **3 — Moderate**
   * **4 — High**
   * **5 — Very high**
8. Explain each score using evidence from the supplied materials.
9. Calculate a weighted overall score only when approved weights are provided. Do not invent weights.
10. Consider portfolio constraints, including funding, staffing, technology capacity, leadership attention, required sequencing, and change-management capacity.
11. Rank the initiatives based on the evidence, scores, mandatory requirements, dependencies, and constraints.
12. Recommend one disposition for each initiative:

    * Accelerate.
    * Proceed.
    * Sequence.
    * Revise.
    * Defer.
    * Combine.
    * Pause.
    * Discontinue.
    * Escalate for leadership decision.
13. Identify portfolio conflicts, strategic coverage gaps, resource constraints, and consequences of delaying high-priority work.
14. Identify leadership decisions required, decision owners, deadlines, trade-offs, and consequences of delay.
15. Present the analysis using the structure defined in `OUTPUTS.md`.

## Prioritization principles

* Mandatory work should be identified separately rather than automatically ranked above all discretionary work.
* A high strategic-alignment score does not automatically mean an initiative is feasible or ready.
* A low-cost initiative is not necessarily a high priority.
* A high-risk initiative may require mitigation, redesign, sequencing, or escalation rather than rejection.
* Missing evidence should reduce confidence in the recommendation, not automatically reduce the initiative’s score.
* Dependencies and resource constraints may change the recommended sequence even when an initiative has a high score.
* Portfolio balance should be considered in addition to individual initiative scores.

## Guardrails

* Use only approved goals, priorities, criteria, and evidence supplied in the inputs.
* Do not invent benefits, costs, owners, deadlines, dependencies, scores, or approvals.
* Do not assume scoring weights when none are provided.
* Clearly distinguish documented facts, assumptions, analysis, and recommendations.
* Identify the source and effective date of important evidence when available.
* Flag conflicting, incomplete, outdated, or unverified information.
* Do not automatically approve, fund, pause, discontinue, or change an initiative.
* Do not reassign resources, ownership, scope, or timelines.
* Require authorized leadership review before recommendations affect portfolio commitments or resource allocation.
