# Manage Competing Priorities

## Purpose

Help leadership understand and resolve conflicts among important priorities. Identify what is competing, why the conflict exists, which commitments are affected, and what trade-offs accompany each resolution option.

## Instructions

1. Review the materials defined in `INPUTS.md`.
2. Confirm that the strategic goals, priorities, commitments, deadlines, and capacity information are current.
3. Identify situations where two or more priorities compete for:

   * Funding.
   * Staffing.
   * Specialized expertise.
   * Technology or integration capacity.
   * Leadership attention.
   * Delivery time.
   * Shared dependencies.
   * Organizational change capacity.
4. For each conflict, identify:

   * Priorities and initiatives involved.
   * Strategic goals affected.
   * Executive sponsors and accountable owners.
   * Source of the conflict.
   * Required resolution date.
   * Commitments at risk.
5. Separate mandatory work from discretionary work, but do not assume all mandatory work must receive unlimited resources.
6. Compare each competing priority using:

   * Strategic importance.
   * Expected organizational value.
   * Urgency.
   * Consequence of delay.
   * Resource requirements.
   * Current progress.
   * Dependencies.
   * Risk of proceeding.
   * Risk of delaying or stopping.
   * Reversibility.
7. Identify whether the conflict is genuine or can be resolved through coordination, sequencing, scope clarification, or removal of duplicate work.
8. Determine whether teams or resources are committed beyond available capacity.
9. Develop evidence-based resolution options, such as:

   * Sequence the priorities.
   * Reallocate resources.
   * Adjust scope.
   * Extend a deadline.
   * Defer or pause lower-value work.
   * Combine overlapping work.
   * Resolve a shared dependency.
   * Add capacity.
   * Escalate for leadership direction.
10. For each option, explain:

    * Benefits.
    * Costs and trade-offs.
    * Risks.
    * Dependencies.
    * Effect on existing commitments.
    * Reversibility.
11. Recommend an option only when supported by sufficient evidence.
12. Identify leadership decisions required, decision owners, deadlines, and consequences of delay.
13. Present the analysis using the structure defined in `OUTPUTS.md`.

## Priority-management principles

* The newest request is not automatically the highest priority.
* Urgency and strategic importance are different considerations.
* Mandatory work should be identified clearly, not automatically exempted from scope or resource review.
* Partially completed work should not continue solely because resources have already been invested.
* A high-value priority may still need to be sequenced if capacity or dependencies are unavailable.
* Resolving a conflict may require changing scope or timing rather than selecting one priority and rejecting another.
* Missing evidence should reduce confidence in the recommendation, not automatically reduce a priority’s importance.

## Guardrails

* Use only approved priorities, commitments, criteria, and evidence supplied in the inputs.
* Do not invent resource availability, deadlines, owners, benefits, dependencies, or approvals.
* Do not assume scoring weights when none are provided.
* Clearly distinguish facts, assumptions, analysis, and recommendations.
* Cite the source and date supporting significant findings when available.
* Flag incomplete, conflicting, outdated, or unverified information.
* Do not automatically change priorities, commitments, funding, staffing, scope, ownership, or timelines.
* Do not communicate recommended changes as approved decisions.
* Require authorized leadership review before any resolution option is implemented.
