# Skill: Overview Of Work Week Ahead

## Purpose

This skill synthesizes the executive’s calendar, priorities, decisions, deliverables, commitments, risks, stakeholder needs, and available capacity into a coherent view of the upcoming work week. It helps leadership understand what must be accomplished, when executive attention is needed, where conflicts may arise, and how each day contributes to the week’s intended outcomes.

The skill prepares a recommended weekly operating plan. It does not independently modify calendars, assign work, approve decisions, communicate commitments, or change organizational priorities.

## Core concepts

### Weekly outcomes versus weekly activities

- **Activities** describe meetings and work performed.
- **Outcomes** describe the results the week must produce.
- The weekly overview should explain what success looks like, not simply repeat the calendar.

### Weekly plan versus calendar summary

- A **calendar summary** lists scheduled events.
- A **weekly plan** connects events, preparation, decisions, deliverables, risks, stakeholders, and follow-up to intended outcomes.
- Calendar events should be included because of their relevance, not merely because they exist.

### Fixed and flexible work

- **Fixed work** is tied to nonmovable deadlines, external commitments, required participants, or formal governance processes.
- **Flexible work** may be rescheduled, delegated, shortened, combined, or completed asynchronously.
- Flexibility must be assessed using purpose and consequence, not meeting type alone.

### Priority and readiness

- **Priority** reflects value, consequence, urgency, and need for executive involvement.
- **Readiness** reflects whether the evidence, people, authority, and prerequisites are available.
- A high-priority item may require early preparation because it is not yet ready.

### Cross-day continuity

A strong weekly plan shows how:

- Early-week preparation supports later meetings.
- One decision affects subsequent deliverables.
- Actions and commitments move between days.
- Risks and dependencies evolve across the week.
- Work this week prepares the following week.

## Core activities

### 1. Establish the weekly planning horizon

- Confirm the week’s dates, working hours, travel, leave, and major constraints.
- Identify the executive’s current strategic and operational context.
- Review unresolved items carried forward from the previous week.
- Identify upcoming milestones beyond the week that require advance preparation.

### 2. Gather weekly commitments

Review:

- Calendar events.
- Strategic and operational priorities.
- Decisions and approvals.
- Deliverables and milestones.
- Actions and commitments.
- Risks, issues, and blockers.
- Stakeholder engagements.
- Preparation and follow-up requirements.

Consolidate duplicate or overlapping items.

### 3. Define the week’s primary outcomes

- Convert activity lists into clear weekly outcomes.
- Connect each outcome to a documented objective, commitment, or responsibility.
- Identify the executive’s required contribution.
- Define an observable success condition.
- Limit the primary outcomes to a realistic number.

### 4. Assess priority and consequence

For each candidate priority, evaluate:

- Strategic alignment.
- Deadline and urgency.
- Consequence of delay.
- Executive-specific authority or contribution.
- Risk reduction.
- Stakeholder importance.
- Effect on other priorities or milestones.
- Readiness and effort required.

Explain the rationale rather than relying only on a numerical score.

### 5. Map dependencies across the week

- Identify work that must happen before a meeting, decision, or milestone.
- Connect prior decisions to upcoming actions.
- Identify people, evidence, approvals, or systems required.
- Surface dependencies shared by multiple initiatives.
- Flag sequencing conflicts or work scheduled too late to support its intended outcome.

### 6. Develop the day-by-day plan

- Assign a primary outcome to each day.
- Place fixed meetings and deadlines.
- Add required preparation before important events.
- Protect focus time for significant work.
- Include follow-up, travel, transition, and breaks.
- Preserve contingency capacity.
- Avoid placing more work into a day than available time permits.

### 7. Assess meeting and decision readiness

For each significant meeting or decision, determine whether:

- The objective is clear.
- Required participants and authority are available.
- Supporting information is current.
- Options and trade-offs are sufficiently developed.
- Prior decisions and actions are represented accurately.
- Risks and dependencies are visible.
- Preparation can be completed on time.

Recommend corrective preparation where readiness is incomplete.

### 8. Identify conflicts and overload

- Compare planned effort with available capacity.
- Identify overlapping meetings and deadlines.
- Flag days dominated by meetings with insufficient preparation or follow-up.
- Identify priorities competing for the same people or resources.
- Surface work that cannot realistically be completed.
- Recommend explicit trade-offs.

### 9. Recommend delegation and adjustments

When conflicts exist:

- Identify work that may be delegated, deferred, shortened, combined, or completed asynchronously.
- Explain the expected benefit and potential consequence.
- Identify a proposed delegate or alternative date when supported.
- Clarify authority and context required.
- Preserve human approval for any assignment or calendar change.

### 10. Surface executive attention points

Highlight:

- Decisions with approaching deadlines.
- Risks or issues exceeding thresholds.
- Unready high-priority meetings.
- Stakeholder commitments at risk.
- Deliverables with weak evidence or unclear ownership.
- Cross-functional conflicts requiring leadership judgment.
- Items likely to affect the following week.

### 11. Define weekly success measures

- State the observable outcome for each primary priority.
- Identify required decisions, deliverables, communications, or risk responses.
- Clarify who confirms completion.
- Identify evidence to preserve.
- Connect unfinished work to the next planning cycle.

## Decision rules

- Do not treat every calendar event as a weekly priority.
- Do not classify an item as high priority solely because a senior stakeholder requested it.
- Give greater weight to work requiring unique executive authority or judgment.
- Include preparation and follow-up when estimating workload.
- Do not schedule dependent work after the decision or meeting it is intended to support.
- Do not represent tentative events, proposed deadlines, or expected decisions as confirmed.
- Do not hide overload by distributing unrealistic work across multiple days.
- Preserve contingency capacity whenever reasonably possible.
- Recommend delegation only when authority, capacity, context, and required expertise can be confirmed.
- Carry unresolved commitments forward with their original source and history.
- Identify when a high-priority decision should be delayed because material evidence or required review is missing.

## Quality standards

A high-quality weekly overview must be:

- **Outcome-oriented:** centered on what the week must accomplish.
- **Prioritized:** distinguishes essential work from routine activity.
- **Integrated:** connects meetings, decisions, deliverables, risks, and stakeholders.
- **Sequenced:** preparation and dependencies occur at the appropriate time.
- **Realistic:** planned effort fits within available capacity.
- **Traceable:** material claims connect to source records.
- **Current:** reflects recent changes and unresolved prior work.
- **Decision-ready:** makes authority, readiness, options, and deadlines visible.
- **Continuous:** prepares both the current week and the next planning cycle.
- **Human-reviewed:** recommendations do not become commitments automatically.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not independently create, move, accept, decline, or cancel calendar events.
- Do not assign or delegate work without authorized confirmation.
- Do not fabricate priorities, deadlines, owners, readiness, or strategic alignment.
- Do not represent recommendations or anticipated outcomes as approved decisions.
- Do not expose confidential meeting, personnel, clinical, financial, security, privacy, or governance information to unauthorized audiences.
- Do not make specialist determinations requiring qualified review.
- Do not infer stakeholder importance or decision authority solely from title.
- Do not suppress uncertainty, missing evidence, or unresolved conflicts.
- Clearly distinguish confirmed commitments from proposed plans.

## Human role

The executive or authorized reviewer must:

- Confirm the week’s primary outcomes.
- Validate priorities, consequences, and strategic alignment.
- Approve calendar changes, delegation, deferral, or declined work.
- Confirm sensitive stakeholder and organizational considerations.
- Make or authorize required decisions.
- Resolve competing priorities and capacity conflicts.
- Review results and determine what carries into the following week.
