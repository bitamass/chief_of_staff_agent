# Skill: Draft New Tasks

## Purpose

This skill converts documented requests, decisions, commitments, meeting outcomes, risks, issues, and planning needs into clear draft task records. It helps ensure that proposed work has a specific outcome, appropriate owner, realistic timing, completion criteria, dependencies, authority boundaries, and source traceability before being assigned or added to an official tracking system.

The skill drafts tasks for review. It does not create, assign, activate, close, or communicate tasks without authorized human approval.

## Core concepts

### Task versus note

- A **note** preserves information.
- A **task** requires a person to produce a defined result.
- Statements without an action, outcome, or responsible role should not automatically become tasks.

### Task versus decision

- A **task** describes work to be completed.
- A **decision** selects or authorizes a direction.
- If a decision is still required, the task should prepare or facilitate the decision rather than imply that it has already been made.

### Task versus commitment

- A **task** may be proposed or assigned.
- A **commitment** indicates that an authorized person has agreed to deliver something.
- A draft task must not be represented as a commitment until the appropriate person confirms it.

### Activity versus outcome

- An **activity** describes effort, such as “review the report.”
- An **outcome** describes the intended result, such as “determine whether the report is ready for executive approval.”
- Strong tasks identify the outcome as well as the activity.

### Task readiness

- **Ready:** outcome, owner, deadline, inputs, and authority are sufficiently defined.
- **Partially ready:** useful work can begin, but material clarification is still needed.
- **Not ready:** the task cannot reasonably begin or achieve its intended outcome.
- **Awaiting information:** activation depends on a named input, decision, or person.

## Core activities

### 1. Identify potential work

Review available sources for:

- Explicit requests.
- Decisions that require implementation.
- Commitments requiring follow-through.
- Meeting actions.
- Risks requiring mitigation.
- Issues requiring resolution.
- Milestones or deliverables requiring preparation.
- Missing work needed to achieve a documented objective.

Do not convert general discussion, speculation, or every recommendation into a task.

### 2. Confirm the task is necessary

Determine whether the proposed work:

- Produces a distinct result.
- Supports a documented objective or obligation.
- Is not already complete.
- Is not already represented by an existing task.
- Requires action rather than only awareness.
- Has sufficient value to justify tracking.
- Falls within the appropriate organizational scope.

### 3. Draft an action-oriented title

The task title should:

- Begin with a clear action verb.
- Identify the principal object or result.
- Be understandable outside the original conversation.
- Avoid vague wording such as “handle,” “look into,” or “follow up” without additional specificity.
- Avoid embedding unsupported decisions or commitments.

Example:

- Weak: “Security follow-up.”
- Strong: “Document identity and access requirements for the phase-one pilot.”

### 4. Define the required outcome

- Explain what successful completion will produce.
- Identify why the work matters.
- Connect it to the relevant initiative, decision, meeting, or commitment.
- Describe the evidence required to verify completion.
- Distinguish a final deliverable from interim progress.

### 5. Establish ownership and accountability

- Identify a proposed responsible owner.
- Identify the accountable person when different.
- Specify contributors, reviewers, and approvers.
- Confirm that the owner has or can receive the required authority, access, expertise, and capacity.
- Mark all proposed assignments as unconfirmed until accepted.

### 6. Establish timing and priority

- Use documented deadlines when available.
- Distinguish confirmed deadlines from proposed dates.
- Consider required preparation and review time.
- Identify the consequence of delay.
- Compare the task with existing priorities and capacity.
- Avoid inventing urgency to fill missing information.

### 7. Define scope and boundaries

- Identify included and excluded work.
- Specify required level of detail.
- Clarify decisions the owner may make.
- Identify required approvals and specialist reviews.
- State prohibited actions and sensitive information restrictions.
- Keep the task appropriately sized for effective ownership and tracking.

### 8. Identify dependencies and blockers

- List required predecessor work, information, people, systems, and approvals.
- Identify downstream work affected by completion.
- Surface assumptions that could affect execution.
- Classify the task’s readiness.
- Recommend clarification or prerequisite actions when the task is not ready.

### 9. Detect duplicates and overlaps

Compare the proposed task with existing tasks by:

- Outcome.
- Scope.
- Owner.
- Source.
- Due date.
- Related initiative or decision.

Recommend whether to merge, link, revise, or retain tasks separately. Do not remove an existing task automatically.

### 10. Validate task quality

Before recommending activation, confirm that the task has:

- A specific title.
- A meaningful outcome.
- A proposed owner.
- A supported due date or clearly marked proposed date.
- Completion criteria.
- Necessary context.
- Dependencies and risks.
- Authority boundaries.
- Source traceability.
- A reasonable level of effort.

### 11. Prepare for human approval

- Present the draft clearly.
- Highlight assumptions and missing information.
- Ask focused clarification questions.
- Recommend whether to approve, revise, hold, merge, or discard the task.
- Preserve human authority over assignment and activation.

## Decision rules

- Do not create a task from a statement that lacks a required action or outcome.
- Do not treat a recommendation as an approved task.
- Do not identify a person as the owner solely because the person attended the source meeting.
- Do not invent deadlines, priorities, authority, or acceptance criteria.
- Do not create separate tasks when one appropriately scoped task would cover the work.
- Do not combine distinct outcomes under one vague task.
- Use one accountable outcome per task whenever practical.
- Label proposed owners and dates as proposed until confirmed.
- If the work depends on an unresolved decision, draft a decision-preparation task rather than an implementation task.
- If sensitive or regulated review is required, include it explicitly in the task boundaries and completion criteria.
- If essential information is missing, recommend holding the task for clarification rather than producing false precision.

## Quality standards

A high-quality draft task must be:

- **Specific:** the required result is unambiguous.
- **Action-oriented:** the title begins with meaningful work.
- **Outcome-based:** completion produces a defined result.
- **Owned:** responsibility and accountability are identifiable.
- **Time-bound:** timing is documented or clearly proposed.
- **Measurable:** completion can be verified.
- **Scoped:** included and excluded work are clear.
- **Ready:** prerequisites and authority are sufficiently addressed.
- **Traceable:** source, assumptions, and related commitments are visible.
- **Nonduplicative:** overlap with existing work has been evaluated.
- **Reviewable:** a human can approve, revise, or reject the draft efficiently.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not independently create or assign tasks in an external system.
- Do not communicate a draft task as an accepted commitment.
- Do not fabricate owners, deadlines, priorities, approvals, or source information.
- Do not infer authority from job title alone.
- Do not expose sensitive source material beyond the authorized audience.
- Do not convert confidential discussion into broadly visible task content without review.
- Do not make legal, clinical, security, privacy, compliance, financial, procurement, or personnel determinations.
- Do not delete, merge, close, or supersede existing tasks without authorization.
- Clearly distinguish confirmed attributes from proposed attributes.

## Human role

An authorized human reviewer must:

- Confirm that the task is necessary.
- Approve the outcome, scope, priority, and deadline.
- Select and confirm the owner.
- Validate authority, access, capacity, and specialist-review requirements.
- Resolve duplicate or conflicting work.
- Authorize creation in the official task-management system.
- Communicate the assignment and obtain acknowledgment.
