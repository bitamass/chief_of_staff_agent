# Skill: Daily Priorities

## Purpose

This skill identifies, evaluates, and ranks the outcomes that most need executive attention during the current day. It combines strategic goals, calendar demands, decisions, commitments, deadlines, risks, stakeholder needs, dependencies, and available capacity to create a focused and realistic daily priority plan.

The skill recommends priorities and trade-offs. It does not independently change commitments, issue approvals, delegate work, communicate decisions, or determine organizational strategy.

## Core concepts

### Outcome versus activity

- An **activity** describes work being performed.
- An **outcome** describes the result that the work must produce.
- Daily priorities should be expressed as outcomes whenever possible.

Example:

- Activity: Review the investment proposal.
- Outcome: Determine whether the investment proposal is ready for phase-one approval.

### Priority versus urgency

- **Priority** reflects strategic value, consequence, and need for executive involvement.
- **Urgency** reflects how soon something requires attention.
- An urgent request is not automatically a high priority if it has limited value or can be handled by someone else.

### Executive-specific work

An item warrants executive attention when it requires:

- Formal decision or approval authority.
- Leadership judgment.
- Sponsorship or organizational alignment.
- Resolution of a cross-functional conflict.
- High-sensitivity stakeholder communication.
- Acceptance of material risk.
- Allocation of significant resources.
- Removal of a barrier that others cannot resolve.

### Daily priority classifications

- **Critical:** must be addressed today to avoid material harm, missed authority, or a nonrecoverable deadline.
- **High:** materially advances an important objective and should be addressed today.
- **Standard:** valuable work that may be completed if capacity remains.
- **Defer:** can move to another date with manageable consequences.
- **Delegate:** should be assigned to an appropriately authorized person.
- **Decline:** does not sufficiently support current objectives or justify the required effort.

### Readiness

A high-value item may not be ready for executive action. Priority and readiness must be evaluated separately:

- **Ready:** evidence, authority, and participants are available.
- **Partially ready:** material gaps remain but useful progress is possible.
- **Not ready:** the requested outcome cannot reasonably be achieved.
- **Awaiting information:** progress depends on a named input or person.

## Core activities

### 1. Gather candidate priorities

- Review strategic priorities, calendar events, decisions, approvals, commitments, risks, issues, and stakeholder requests.
- Carry forward unfinished priorities from the prior day.
- Identify new items created by recent meetings or changes.
- Consolidate duplicate or closely related requests.
- Exclude items that are already complete or no longer relevant.

### 2. Define the required outcome

For each candidate:

- State what must be accomplished.
- Identify why it matters today.
- Confirm the executive’s required role.
- Identify the deadline and consequence of delay.
- Define a reasonable completion or progress condition.
- Distinguish the underlying outcome from the requested activity.

### 3. Evaluate strategic contribution

Assess:

- Connection to approved strategic goals.
- Contribution to initiative or operational outcomes.
- Material value or benefit.
- Importance to executive responsibilities.
- Effect on upcoming milestones and decisions.
- Ability to unblock other work.
- Consistency with prior decisions and commitments.

Do not invent strategic alignment when no documented connection exists.

### 4. Evaluate timing and consequence

Determine:

- Whether a deadline is fixed or flexible.
- Whether delay creates financial, operational, legal, clinical, security, privacy, stakeholder, or reputational consequences.
- Whether the opportunity will remain available later.
- Whether another meeting or milestone depends on completion.
- Whether urgency is externally imposed, internally created, or unsupported.

### 5. Evaluate executive necessity

Ask:

- Does the item require the executive’s authority or judgment?
- Could an authorized person complete it?
- Can the executive contribute during an already scheduled meeting?
- Is a full review necessary, or would a concise decision package be sufficient?
- Would delaying executive attention block multiple people or initiatives?

### 6. Assess readiness and effort

- Confirm required evidence, people, and approvals.
- Estimate preparation and completion time.
- Identify missing inputs and blockers.
- Determine whether meaningful progress is possible today.
- Avoid ranking an unready item above ready work without explaining why.

### 7. Rank competing priorities

Rank items using documented criteria:

- Strategic value.
- Urgency.
- Consequence of delay.
- Risk reduction.
- Stakeholder impact.
- Executive-specific contribution.
- Dependency and unblocking effect.
- Readiness.
- Required effort.
- Delegation or deferral options.

The rationale should be understandable without relying solely on a numerical score.

### 8. Fit priorities to available capacity

- Compare total effort with available working time.
- Account for meetings, preparation, transitions, and follow-up.
- Select a realistic number of primary priorities.
- Preserve contingency time for emerging needs.
- Explicitly identify items that do not fit.
- Do not present an overloaded list as a feasible daily plan.

### 9. Recommend trade-offs

When all work cannot be completed:

- Identify what should be delegated, deferred, combined, shortened, or declined.
- Explain the benefit and consequence of the recommendation.
- Identify any stakeholder communication required.
- Propose a delegate or revised date when appropriate.
- Preserve human approval for changes to commitments.

### 10. Define success and continuity

- Specify what meaningful progress looks like for each priority.
- Capture decisions, approvals, actions, and commitments resulting from the work.
- Identify items that must carry forward.
- Record why unfinished work remains incomplete.
- Connect the daily plan to subsequent meetings and weekly priorities.

## Decision rules

- Rank outcomes, not the volume of activity associated with them.
- Do not classify an item as critical solely because a senior person requested it.
- Give greater weight to work requiring unique executive authority or judgment.
- Treat unsupported urgency as a claim requiring validation.
- Do not recommend deferring an item without considering the consequence and stakeholder commitment.
- Do not recommend delegation without identifying appropriate authority and context.
- Separate importance from readiness.
- Identify when a decision should be delayed because material evidence or required review is missing.
- Preserve prior commitments unless an authorized person approves a change.
- Limit the top-priority list to what can realistically be advanced during the available day.

## Quality standards

A high-quality daily-priority plan must be:

- **Focused:** limited to the most consequential outcomes.
- **Strategically grounded:** connected to documented goals and responsibilities.
- **Realistic:** achievable within available capacity.
- **Explicit:** clear about deadlines, ownership, and required executive action.
- **Comparable:** competing items are assessed using consistent criteria.
- **Transparent:** rationale, uncertainty, and trade-offs are visible.
- **Actionable:** every priority has a next action and success condition.
- **Traceable:** commitments, deadlines, and claims connect to sources.
- **Continuous:** unfinished work and resulting commitments carry into future planning.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not fabricate priorities, deadlines, consequences, authority, or strategic alignment.
- Do not independently accept, reject, delegate, defer, or cancel commitments.
- Do not issue approvals or communicate executive decisions.
- Do not rank stakeholder importance solely by title or organizational level.
- Do not expose confidential priorities or sensitive information to unauthorized audiences.
- Do not present estimates or assumptions as confirmed facts.
- Do not make clinical, legal, security, privacy, compliance, or financial determinations requiring qualified review.
- Clearly identify incomplete evidence, conflicting requests, and low-confidence assessments.

## Human role

The executive or authorized reviewer must:

- Confirm the day’s most important outcomes.
- Validate strategic alignment and material consequences.
- Approve trade-offs, delegation, deferral, or declined work.
- Confirm sensitive stakeholder considerations.
- Resolve conflicts among competing organizational priorities.
- Authorize decisions, communications, and changes to commitments.
- Review end-of-day results and determine what carries forward.
