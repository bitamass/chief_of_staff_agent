# Skill: Define Weekly Priorities

## Purpose

This skill evaluates proposed work for the upcoming week and recommends a focused, realistic set of priorities based on strategic alignment, expected value, deadlines, consequences, risks, stakeholder needs, dependencies, readiness, and available capacity. It helps leadership choose what must be accomplished, what can be delegated or deferred, and what should not compete for attention during the week.

The skill recommends a weekly priority plan. It does not establish organizational strategy, change commitments, assign work, modify calendars, or communicate priorities without authorized human approval.

## Core concepts

### Priority versus task

- A **task** is a defined unit of work.
- A **priority** is an outcome that merits preferential attention and resources.
- Several tasks may support one priority.
- Not every open task should become a weekly priority.

### Outcome versus activity

- An **activity** describes what people will do.
- An **outcome** describes what will be achieved or meaningfully advanced.
- Weekly priorities should be stated as outcomes.

Example:

- Activity: Review the pilot proposal.
- Outcome: Determine whether the pilot proposal is ready for concept validation.

### Importance versus urgency

- **Importance** reflects strategic value and consequence.
- **Urgency** reflects how soon action is required.
- Urgent requests should not automatically displace strategically important work without considering the consequence and ability to delegate.

### Priority versus readiness

- **Priority** indicates how much the outcome matters.
- **Readiness** indicates whether meaningful progress is possible.
- High-priority but unready work may require a readiness-building outcome for the week.

### Weekly priority classifications

- **Critical:** must be addressed during the week to prevent material harm or loss of a nonrecoverable opportunity.
- **High:** should be meaningfully advanced because of its strategic value or consequence.
- **Standard:** valuable work that may proceed after higher priorities are protected.
- **Delegate:** important work that does not require direct executive ownership.
- **Defer:** work that can move to a later period with manageable consequences.
- **Stop:** work that no longer supports current objectives or justifies continued effort.

### Committed capacity

A credible weekly plan must account for:

- Meetings.
- Preparation.
- Focus work.
- Reviews and decisions.
- Communications.
- Travel and transitions.
- Follow-up.
- Routine operating responsibilities.
- Contingency time.

## Core activities

### 1. Establish the weekly context

- Confirm the week’s planning horizon and available working capacity.
- Review approved strategy, current objectives, and executive responsibilities.
- Identify major deadlines, meetings, milestones, and operating conditions.
- Review unfinished priorities and commitments from the previous week.
- Identify near-term work beyond the week that requires advance preparation.

### 2. Gather candidate priorities

Review:

- Strategic objectives and initiatives.
- Calendar commitments.
- Decisions and approvals.
- Deliverables and milestones.
- Actions and stakeholder commitments.
- Risks, issues, and blockers.
- New requests and changing conditions.
- Work carried forward from the previous week.

Consolidate duplicate or overlapping items.

### 3. Define each required outcome

For every candidate:

- State the outcome to achieve during the week.
- Explain why it matters now.
- Identify the accountable owner and executive contribution.
- Define an observable success condition.
- Identify the deadline and consequence of delay.
- Separate the weekly outcome from the full long-term objective.

### 4. Evaluate strategic alignment

Assess whether the candidate:

- Directly advances an approved goal or objective.
- Supports a current initiative or operational responsibility.
- Fulfills a documented commitment.
- Removes a material barrier.
- Reduces significant risk.
- Prepares an upcoming decision or milestone.
- Produces value proportionate to the effort.

Do not invent strategic alignment when it is not documented.

### 5. Evaluate timing and consequence

Determine:

- Whether the deadline is fixed or flexible.
- Whether delay creates material operational, financial, stakeholder, security, privacy, clinical, compliance, legal, workforce, or reputational consequences.
- Whether the opportunity remains available later.
- Whether downstream work depends on completion.
- Whether stated urgency is supported by evidence.

### 6. Evaluate executive necessity

Ask:

- Does the work require unique executive authority or judgment?
- Can another authorized person lead it?
- Can the executive contribute through an existing meeting or concise review?
- Would executive attention unblock multiple people or initiatives?
- Is the work primarily operational and suitable for delegation?

### 7. Assess readiness and feasibility

- Confirm required evidence, people, authority, access, and prerequisites.
- Estimate executive and team effort.
- Identify blockers and dependencies.
- Determine what meaningful progress is possible within one week.
- Define a smaller readiness outcome when full completion is unrealistic.
- Avoid ranking work as executable when material prerequisites are absent.

### 8. Rank competing priorities

Use consistent criteria:

- Strategic alignment.
- Expected value.
- Urgency.
- Consequence of delay.
- Risk reduction.
- Stakeholder impact.
- Executive-specific contribution.
- Dependency and unblocking effect.
- Readiness.
- Effort.
- Reversibility.
- Opportunity window.

Explain the ranking rationale in plain language, even when a numerical method is used.

### 9. Fit the priorities to capacity

- Compare estimated effort with actual available time.
- Account for meeting, preparation, review, and follow-up commitments.
- Limit the primary-priority list to what can realistically be advanced.
- Preserve contingency capacity.
- Identify priorities that cannot fit.
- Do not make the plan appear feasible by omitting required effort.

### 10. Recommend trade-offs

When candidate work exceeds capacity:

- Recommend delegation, deferral, combination, scope reduction, routine handling, or stopping.
- Explain the benefit and consequence of each recommendation.
- Identify a proposed owner or later review point.
- Identify affected stakeholders and communication needs.
- Preserve human authority over changes to commitments.

### 11. Define weekly success

For each selected priority:

- Specify the intended weekly outcome.
- Identify the owner and required executive contribution.
- Define evidence of completion or meaningful progress.
- Identify the acceptance authority.
- Connect the result to subsequent decisions, meetings, and priorities.
- Clarify what will carry into the next planning cycle.

## Decision rules

- Do not make every important initiative a weekly priority.
- Limit weekly priorities to outcomes that can be materially advanced.
- Do not rank an item highly solely because a senior stakeholder requested it.
- Give greater weight to work requiring unique executive authority, judgment, or sponsorship.
- Do not treat unsupported urgency as confirmed.
- Do not hide recurring operational work when it materially affects capacity.
- Separate importance from readiness.
- Define readiness-building work when a high-priority outcome cannot yet proceed.
- Do not recommend delegation without considering authority, expertise, access, and capacity.
- Do not defer a commitment without identifying the consequence and required authorization.
- Preserve prior commitments and source history when priorities change.
- Make overload and trade-offs explicit.

## Quality standards

A high-quality weekly priority plan must be:

- **Focused:** limited to the most consequential outcomes.
- **Strategically grounded:** connected to approved objectives and responsibilities.
- **Outcome-based:** defines results rather than activity lists.
- **Comparable:** applies consistent criteria to competing work.
- **Realistic:** fits within available executive and team capacity.
- **Sequenced:** accounts for dependencies and timing.
- **Transparent:** makes rationale, assumptions, and trade-offs visible.
- **Measurable:** includes observable weekly success conditions.
- **Traceable:** connects priorities to source records.
- **Human-reviewed:** recommendations do not become official priorities automatically.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not independently establish, approve, announce, assign, defer, or cancel priorities.
- Do not fabricate strategic alignment, value, urgency, owners, deadlines, capacity, or consequences.
- Do not represent a proposed weekly plan as an approved leadership commitment.
- Do not infer authority or capability solely from job titles.
- Do not expose sensitive planning, personnel, clinical, financial, security, privacy, or governance information to unauthorized audiences.
- Do not make specialist determinations requiring qualified review.
- Do not remove risks, blockers, dissenting views, or uncertainty to simplify the plan.
- Clearly distinguish confirmed commitments from recommendations and assumptions.

## Human role

The executive or authorized leadership team must:

- Confirm the week’s strategic and operational context.
- Select and approve the final weekly priorities.
- Validate relative value, urgency, and consequences.
- Approve delegation, deferral, scope reduction, or stopping work.
- Confirm owners, capacity, and deadlines.
- Resolve conflicts among initiatives and stakeholders.
- Communicate the approved weekly direction.
- Review weekly results and determine what carries forward.
