# Skill: Initiative Prioritization

## Purpose

This skill evaluates approved and proposed initiatives as a portfolio and recommends how they should be ranked, sequenced, funded, accelerated, deferred, consolidated, paused, rescoped, or stopped.

It helps leadership determine:

- Which initiatives best support current strategy.
- Which initiatives provide the greatest expected value.
- Which work is mandatory or time-sensitive.
- Which initiatives are ready to proceed.
- Which initiatives compete for the same resources.
- Which dependencies affect sequencing.
- Which active initiatives remain worthy of continued investment.
- What trade-offs are required under realistic capacity constraints.

This skill supports portfolio decisions. It does not authorize funding, terminate initiatives, reassign resources, or change approved priorities automatically.

## Core activities

### 1. Define the portfolio decision

Confirm:

- Portfolio scope.
- Planning period.
- Available capacity.
- Decision authority.
- Required decision date.
- Mandatory commitments.
- Approved criteria.
- Strategic context.

Do not rank initiatives without knowing the resource and decision constraints.

### 2. Validate initiatives

For each initiative:

- Confirm objective and scope.
- Confirm sponsor and owner.
- Confirm strategic connection.
- Confirm expected value.
- Confirm cost and resources.
- Confirm readiness.
- Confirm risks and dependencies.
- Confirm current performance for active work.
- Identify missing or weak evidence.

### 3. Separate mandatory and discretionary work

Mandatory initiatives may arise from:

- Law or regulation.
- Contractual obligation.
- Safety or continuity.
- Security or privacy.
- Clinical or operational necessity.
- Formal executive or board commitment.

Mandatory work should still be evaluated for scope, sequencing, efficiency, and delivery risk, but should not be compared with discretionary work as though it can simply be omitted.

### 4. Assess strategic alignment

Evaluate:

- Direct contribution to an approved objective.
- Importance of the objective.
- Strength of the causal connection.
- Executive commitment.
- Duplication or conflict.
- Durability of strategic relevance.

Do not award high alignment because an initiative uses similar language to the strategy.

### 5. Assess expected value

Consider:

- Outcome magnitude.
- Number and importance of beneficiaries.
- Time to value.
- Duration of value.
- Financial and nonfinancial benefit.
- Risk reduction.
- Capability created.
- Evidence and confidence.

Distinguish forecast value from realized value.

### 6. Assess urgency

Consider:

- External deadlines.
- Cost of delay.
- Window of opportunity.
- Dependency timing.
- Operational need.
- Regulatory or contractual timing.
- Whether urgency is evidence-based or self-declared.

Urgency should not be confused with organizational importance.

### 7. Assess readiness

Evaluate:

- Scope.
- Ownership.
- business case.
- Requirements.
- Resources.
- Technology and data.
- Stakeholder alignment.
- Governance and assurance.
- Dependencies.
- Adoption readiness.

A high-value initiative may be sequenced later if it is not ready.

### 8. Assess effort and feasibility

Consider:

- Cost.
- Staffing.
- Expertise.
- Technology.
- Vendor requirements.
- Complexity.
- Duration.
- Change impact.
- Operational burden.
- Confidence.

Do not automatically favor small initiatives; consider value relative to effort.

### 9. Assess risk and dependency

Evaluate:

- Residual risk.
- Delivery risk.
- Organizational risk.
- Critical dependencies.
- Shared resources.
- Portfolio concentration.
- Reversibility.
- Contingency options.

High risk does not always mean low priority. Some high-risk initiatives address essential obligations, but they may require stronger controls or phased implementation.
