# Skill: Strategic Alignment Analysis

## Purpose

This skill evaluates whether an initiative’s objectives, scope, deliverables, resources, measures, and expected outcomes remain meaningfully connected to approved organizational strategy.

It helps leadership determine:

* Why the initiative exists.
* Which strategic objectives it supports.
* Whether its current activities and deliverables contribute to those objectives.
* Whether resources are being invested in strategically valuable work.
* Whether outcomes and benefits are measurable.
* Whether changes in strategy or operating context require adjustment.
* Whether the initiative should continue, accelerate, consolidate, rescope, pause, or stop.

Strategic alignment is not established merely because an initiative uses language similar to the strategic plan. The relationship must be explicit, plausible, measurable, and supported by evidence.

## Core concepts

### Strategic priority

An approved organizational direction or outcome established through a strategic plan, executive decision, board decision, governance process, or operating plan.

### Initiative objective

A specific outcome the initiative intends to produce.

### Deliverable

A defined output created to support an initiative objective.

### Outcome

A measurable change resulting from the initiative’s activities and deliverables.

### Benefit

The strategic, operational, financial, clinical, research, workforce, stakeholder, risk-reduction, or capability value produced by an outcome.

### Strategic traceability

The documented relationship connecting organizational strategy to initiative objectives, measures, deliverables, investments, outcomes, and benefits.

## Core activities

### 1. Validate the strategic framework

* Identify the current approved strategy.
* Confirm its version and planning period.
* Identify applicable strategic priorities.
* Identify recent changes in strategy.
* Distinguish approved strategy from informal preferences.
* Record authoritative sources and owners.

Do not evaluate an initiative against outdated, draft, or unapproved strategic language.

### 2. Validate the initiative purpose

Confirm:

* The problem or opportunity being addressed.
* Intended outcomes.
* Executive sponsor.
* Accountable owner.
* Scope and exclusions.
* Intended beneficiaries.
* Success measures.
* Current phase.
* Approved investment.

Flag initiatives whose purpose is vague, activity-based, unsupported, or no longer current.

### 3. Build the strategic-alignment chain

Map:

Strategic priority
→ Initiative objective
→ Success measure
→ Deliverable
→ Resource investment
→ Outcome
→ Benefit

For each connection:

* Identify the source.
* Explain the relationship.
* Assess the strength of the relationship.
* Identify the owner.
* Identify supporting evidence.
* Record uncertainty or missing information.

A deliverable should not be described as strategically aligned when its contribution to an objective is unclear.

### 4. Evaluate strategic contribution

Assess:

* Directness of contribution.
* Materiality of the expected outcome.
* Importance of the strategic priority.
* Strength of evidence.
* Time to value.
* Duration of expected value.
* Organizational influence over the outcome.
* Stakeholder relevance.
* Current performance.
* Confidence in the assessment.

Distinguish nominal, indirect, and material alignment.

### 5. Evaluate scope alignment

Determine whether the current scope:

* Supports the intended objective.
* Includes unnecessary work.
* Omits required work.
* Expanded beyond the approved purpose.
* Conflicts with another initiative.
* Remains proportionate to expected value.

Identify deliverables that should be retained, strengthened, clarified, consolidated, rescoped, removed, or replaced.

### 6. Evaluate measurement alignment

Determine whether measures:

* Reflect the desired outcome.
* Have defined baselines and targets.
* Are attributable enough to support decisions.
* Distinguish activity, output, outcome, and benefit.
* Provide timely and reliable evidence.
* Avoid unintended incentives.

An initiative cannot demonstrate strategic contribution using only activity measures when the strategy requires an organizational outcome.

### 7. Evaluate resource alignment

Assess whether:

* Funding and staffing correspond to strategic importance.
* High-priority outcomes receive sufficient resources.
* Low-contribution work consumes disproportionate capacity.
* Shared resources create conflicts.
* Remaining investment is justified by expected future value.
* Opportunity costs are visible.

Do not use sunk cost alone as justification for continuing an initiative.

### 8. Evaluate stakeholder and obligation alignment

Assess:

* Sponsor engagement.
* Stakeholder needs.
* Operational ownership.
* Adoption.
* Affected groups.
* Mandatory requirements.
* Governance and assurance obligations.
* Whether execution remains valuable and acceptable to intended beneficiaries.

### 9. Evaluate portfolio fit

Identify:

* Duplication.
* Overlap.
* Conflicting objectives.
* Shared capabilities.
* Consolidation opportunities.
* Cross-initiative dependencies.
* Resource conflicts.
* Portfolio gaps.

An initiative may be individually valuable while still being poorly positioned within the broader portfolio.

### 10. Assess changes in strategic context

Determine whether changes in the following affect the initiative:

* Leadership priorities.
* Budget.
* Policy.
* Regulation.
* Technology.
* Stakeholder needs.
* Organizational structure.
* Risk exposure.
* External environment.

Identify whether the changes affect the initiative’s relevance, scope, measures, timing, expected value, or appropriate level of investment.

### 11. Apply the approved alignment methodology

When an approved scoring method exists, evaluate:

* Strategic relevance.
* Directness of contribution.
* Outcome value.
* Strength of evidence.
* Stakeholder value.
* Mandatory obligations.
* Resource proportionality.
* Portfolio fit.
* Future relevance.

Show:

* Criteria.
* Definitions.
* Weights.
* Scores.
* Evidence.
* Confidence.
* Adjustments and their rationale.

A numerical score supports judgment; it does not replace executive decision-making.

### 12. Identify alignment gaps

Surface:

* Objectives disconnected from strategy.
* Deliverables disconnected from objectives.
* Measures that do not reflect outcomes.
* Benefits without evidence.
* Resources assigned to low-contribution work.
* Uncontrolled scope growth.
* Missing mandatory requirements.
* Stakeholder needs no longer reflected.
* Conflicts with other initiatives.
* Missing strategic capabilities.

### 13. Recommend disposition

Possible recommendations include:

* Continue unchanged.
* Continue with stronger measurement.
* Accelerate.
* Rescope.
* Consolidate.
* Reprioritize.
* Sequence later.
* Pause.
* Stop.
* Revalidate after obtaining additional evidence.

Every recommendation should identify:

* Rationale.
* Supporting evidence.
* Benefits.
* Costs.
* Resource implications.
* Risks and trade-offs.
* Required authority.
* Decision deadline.
* Next review date.

## Decision rules

* Use the current approved strategy.
* Require an explicit strategy-to-outcome connection.
* Do not treat shared vocabulary as proof of alignment.
* Distinguish activity, output, outcome, and benefit.
* Evaluate current and future value—not sunk cost alone.
* Consider mandatory obligations separately.
* Evaluate portfolio fit and opportunity cost.
* Do not infer causation without evidence.
* Do not hide weak measurement.
* Identify strategic changes.
* Preserve contradictory evidence.
* Label assumptions and uncertainty.
* Clearly distinguish recommendations from approved decisions.

## Quality standards

A strong strategic-alignment analysis must be:

* Strategy-based.
* Traceable.
* Outcome-focused.
* Evidence-based.
* Portfolio-aware.
* Resource-aware.
* Current.
* Confidence-aware.
* Comparative.
* Actionable.

## Safeguards and limitations

* Do not fabricate strategy, alignment, benefits, outcomes, scores, evidence, or leadership priorities.
* Do not terminate, pause, consolidate, rescope, or reprioritize initiatives automatically.
* Do not present a numerical score as objective truth.
* Do not make formal strategy, budget, investment, governance, or portfolio decisions.
* Do not expose restricted strategic, financial, clinical, personnel, legal, security, privacy, procurement, or vendor information.
* Flag stale, missing, conflicting, and low-confidence evidence.
* Require authorized human and specialized review for consequential recommendations.

## Human role

The executive sponsor, initiative owner, strategy lead, portfolio lead, finance and resource partners, benefits owner, stakeholder representatives, governance partners, and authorized executives must:

* Confirm the current strategy.
* Validate the strategic-alignment chain.
* Review outcome and benefit evidence.
* Confirm resource and portfolio implications.
* Resolve conflicting interpretations.
* Approve any change in scope, priority, investment, sequencing, or initiative disposition.
