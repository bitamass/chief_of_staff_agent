# Skill: Initiative Dependency Management

## Purpose

This skill identifies, maps, monitors, and supports resolution of dependencies among initiatives, programs, deliverables, decisions, teams, systems, vendors, resources, and governance processes.

It helps leadership understand:

- What each initiative needs from another party.
- Who provides and receives the dependency.
- When the input is required.
- Whether it will be ready and acceptable.
- What milestones and outcomes are affected.
- Where shared resources, systems, vendors, or approvals create bottlenecks.
- Which dependencies require coordination, contingency planning, or escalation.
- How one delay may propagate through the portfolio.

A dependency is a required input, condition, decision, capability, resource, approval, or deliverable that another activity or outcome relies upon.

## Core dependency types

### Deliverable dependency

One initiative requires an artifact or output produced by another.

### Decision dependency

Work cannot proceed until an authorized person or body makes a decision.

### Resource dependency

Multiple initiatives rely on limited staffing, funding, equipment, expertise, or executive attention.

### Technology or integration dependency

An initiative requires a platform, interface, environment, architecture decision, or technical capability.

### Data dependency

Work requires data access, quality, classification, governance, integration, or availability.

### Vendor or procurement dependency

Progress relies on a contract, purchase, vendor commitment, external service, or partner deliverable.

### Governance or approval dependency

Work requires security, privacy, legal, clinical, regulatory, financial, policy, architecture, procurement, records-management, or other formal approval.

### Operational-readiness dependency

Implementation requires training, support, documentation, staffing, communication, change management, or operational acceptance.

## Core activities

### 1. Identify dependencies

Extract dependencies from:

- Charters.
- Roadmaps.
- Plans.
- Deliverables.
- Milestones.
- Decisions.
- Meeting notes.
- Risk and issue records.
- Architecture and assurance reviews.
- Contracts and statements of work.
- Action and commitment trackers.

For each dependency:

- Identify the consumer.
- Identify the provider.
- Define what is required.
- Define the required date.
- Identify acceptance criteria.
- Identify the downstream impact.
- Record the source.

Do not use vague statements such as “depends on IT” when the required input and accountable role can be specified.

### 2. Normalize the dependency statement

A strong dependency statement should specify:

> [Dependent initiative] requires [specific input] from [provider] by [date] so that [milestone or outcome] can occur.

Include:

- Owner.
- Provider owner.
- Readiness criteria.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Consequence of delay.
- Current forecast.
- Alternative or workaround.

### 3. Map relationships

Connect:

- Upstream provider.
- Required input.
- Downstream consumer.
- Affected deliverable.
- Affected milestone.
- Further downstream dependencies.

Detect:

- Shared dependencies.
- Dependency chains.
- Circular dependencies.
- Single points of failure.
- Highly connected providers.
- Conflicting required dates.
- Duplicate dependency records.

### 4. Assess criticality

Evaluate:

- Effect on strategic objectives.
- Critical-path status.
- Time available.
- Number of initiatives affected.
- Availability of alternatives.
- Cost of delay.
- Risk and compliance implications.
- Difficulty of recovery.
- Dependency velocity.

High criticality should reflect consequence and recoverability, not visibility alone.

### 5. Monitor readiness

For each dependency:

- Compare the required-by date with the provider’s forecast.
- Review progress evidence.
- Confirm review and approval time.
- Confirm acceptance criteria.
- Identify missing components.
- Identify decisions and blockers.
- Determine whether a partial handoff is useful.
- Track the next checkpoint.

Do not classify a dependency as fulfilled merely because the provider reports completion. The consumer may still need to validate and accept it.

### 6. Distinguish delivery from acceptance

Use these stages:

- **Ready for handoff:** provider believes the input is complete.
- **Delivered:** input was transferred.
- **Pending validation:** consumer is reviewing it.
- **Accepted:** required criteria are met.
- **Rejected:** the input does not meet criteria.
- **Fulfilled:** accepted input has satisfied the dependency.

These stages must not be treated as interchangeable.

### 7. Analyze schedule propagation

When a dependency moves:

- Identify the affected consumer milestone.
- Calculate or estimate schedule impact.
- Identify downstream milestones.
- Determine whether float exists.
- Identify recovery options.
- Update the forecast without overwriting the original baseline.
- Flag decisions required.

Do not assume every upstream delay causes an equal downstream delay; evaluate available float, workarounds, and resequencing.

### 8. Recommend resolution

Possible responses include:

- Clarify the required input.
- Clarify acceptance criteria.
- Align provider and consumer dates.
- Increase monitoring.
- Resolve a pending decision.
- Reallocate resources.
- Use a partial delivery.
- Create a workaround.
- Identify an alternate provider.
- Resequence work.
- Revise scope.
- Use formal change control.
- Escalate to sponsor or executive.

Every recommendation should identify the required action, owner, and date.

### 9. Manage cross-initiative conflicts

When initiatives compete for the same resource, system, vendor, approval, or decision:

- Identify the conflict.
- Show the affected outcomes and dates.
- Identify current priorities.
- Present viable sequencing or allocation options.
- Explain trade-offs.
- Identify the authorized decision-maker.
- Request a decision by a specific date.

The skill should not silently choose one initiative over another.

### 10. Review dependency closure

A dependency may be closed when:

- The required input was delivered.
- Acceptance criteria were met.
- The consumer accepted it.
- Downstream work can proceed.
- Residual actions are recorded.
- The authorized owner confirms closure.

Preserve evidence of fulfillment.

## Decision rules

- Identify both provider and consumer.
- State the required input and required-by date.
- Preserve original dates and current forecasts separately.
- Define readiness and acceptance criteria.
- Distinguish provider completion from consumer acceptance.
- Do not infer acceptance from delivery or silence.
- Do not close a dependency without validation.
- Do not assume every delay propagates equally.
- Distinguish dependencies from risks, issues, assumptions, and actions.
- Do not assign priority between competing initiatives without authority.
- Identify shared and systemic dependencies.
- Escalate to the lowest level capable of resolving the conflict.
- Clearly separate current status from recommended changes.

## Quality standards

A strong dependency-management output must be:

- Specific.
- Bidirectionally owned.
- Time-bound.
- Acceptance-aware.
- Impact-linked.
- Cross-initiative.
- Evidence-based.
- Current.
- Actionable.
- Traceable.

Leadership should be able to understand not only that a dependency exists, but exactly what is needed, from whom, by when, for which outcome, and with what consequence if delayed.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not invent dependencies, owners, dates, acceptance criteria, or impacts.
- Do not modify roadmaps, priorities, resources, or commitments automatically.
- Do not make unauthorized cross-initiative priority decisions.
- Do not treat the generated dependency map as an approved contractual or governance record.
- Do not expose confidential vendor, personnel, security, privacy, clinical, legal, or financial information to unauthorized audiences.
- Flag stale, conflicting, missing, or unsupported data.
- Require human approval for escalation, resequencing, resource allocation, scope change, or dependency closure.

## Human role

The initiative owners, provider and consumer owners, program or portfolio lead, sponsor, governance partner, and executive decision-maker must:

- Validate the dependency.
- Confirm ownership and dates.
- Approve acceptance criteria.
- Resolve cross-initiative conflicts.
- Approve resource or schedule changes.
- Authorize workarounds and escalations.
- Confirm acceptance and closure.
- Maintain the dependency register as plans change.
