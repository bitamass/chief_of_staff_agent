# Skill: Track Deliverables

## Purpose

This skill maintains a source-linked view of deliverables across initiatives, programs, projects, workstreams, and executive priorities.

It helps leadership determine:

- What must be delivered.
- Who owns and approves each deliverable.
- What completion and acceptance mean.
- Whether work is progressing against the approved baseline.
- Which milestones, dependencies, risks, or approvals affect delivery.
- Which deliverables require follow-up, recovery planning, or leadership intervention.
- Whether delivered work has been accepted, implemented, and placed into use.

A deliverable is a defined output or outcome that can be reviewed against documented scope and acceptance criteria. It is not merely an activity, meeting, discussion, or general work effort.

## Core activities

### 1. Identify and normalize deliverables

Extract deliverables from approved:

- Charters.
- Roadmaps.
- Project and program plans.
- Statements of work.
- Decisions.
- Meeting records.
- Action trackers.
- Status reports.
- Contracts and agreements.

For each item:

- Preserve its source.
- Write a clear deliverable name and description.
- Identify its owner, approver, due date, and initiative.
- Capture its required components and acceptance criteria.
- Flag missing or ambiguous information.

Do not convert routine activities into deliverables unless they produce a defined, reviewable output or outcome.

### 2. Establish the baseline

Record:

- Approved scope.
- Original due date.
- Interim milestones.
- Required reviews and approvals.
- Resource assumptions.
- Dependencies.
- Acceptance criteria.
- Expected implementation or handoff.

Preserve the original baseline even when an updated forecast or approved rebaseline exists.

### 3. Assess progress

Evaluate progress using observable evidence:

- Completed components.
- Drafts and versions.
- Review results.
- Approvals.
- Testing or validation.
- Implementation readiness.
- Handoff completion.
- Acceptance by the authorized recipient.

Do not rely solely on self-reported percentages. If percentage complete is used, document the calculation method or supporting evidence.

### 4. Distinguish lifecycle states

Apply lifecycle states consistently:

- **Draft complete:** an initial version exists.
- **Submitted for review:** the deliverable has entered formal review.
- **Pending approval:** review is complete or underway, but required approval is outstanding.
- **Approved:** the authorized approver has accepted the deliverable.
- **Implemented:** the approved deliverable has been put into use.
- **Completed:** all required scope, acceptance, implementation, documentation, and closure conditions have been satisfied.

These states must not be treated as interchangeable.

### 5. Assess schedule status

Compare:

- Original due date.
- Approved revised due date.
- Current forecast.
- Interim milestone performance.
- Time required for review and approval.
- Critical dependencies.
- Effect on downstream work.

A deliverable may be progressing but still be At risk if its current pace, dependencies, or approval timeline threaten the due date.

### 6. Evaluate scope and acceptance

Determine:

- Whether required components remain in scope.
- Whether scope has changed.
- Who approved the change.
- Which acceptance criteria have been satisfied.
- Which evidence is missing.
- Whether the approver has accepted the result.
- Whether residual work prevents closure.

Do not label a deliverable completed because a document or artifact exists if required approval, implementation, or acceptance remains outstanding.

### 7. Analyze dependencies and blockers

For each material dependency:

- Identify the required input.
- Identify its owner.
- State the required date.
- Explain the effect of delay.
- Record the resolution plan.
- Identify escalation requirements.

Distinguish a deliverable-owner delay from an upstream dependency, unresolved decision, resource limitation, or approval delay.

### 8. Recommend intervention

Possible recommendations include:

- Routine monitoring.
- Owner status confirmation.
- Dependency resolution.
- Additional review or evidence.
- Resource adjustment.
- Scope clarification.
- Recovery plan.
- Schedule reforecast.
- Formal rebaseline.
- Sponsor intervention.
- Executive escalation.
- Deferral, cancellation, or supersession.

Recommendations must identify the decision-maker, requested action, and required date.

### 9. Analyze portfolio patterns

Across deliverables, identify:

- Repeated missed milestones.
- Approval bottlenecks.
- Chronic dependencies.
- Scope instability.
- Lack of acceptance criteria.
- Excessive concentration of delivery ownership.
- Resource or expertise gaps.
- Deliverables disconnected from strategic objectives.
- Work completed but not implemented or adopted.
- Duplicate or overlapping deliverables.

## Decision rules

- Do not classify an activity as a deliverable unless it produces a defined output or outcome.
- Preserve the approved baseline and separately show current forecasts.
- Do not overwrite original dates when schedules change.
- Do not report percentage complete without a credible basis.
- Do not mark a deliverable complete without evidence and required acceptance.
- Distinguish draft, submitted, approved, implemented, and completed.
- Do not infer approval from silence or meeting attendance.
- Do not infer acceptance because a file was delivered.
- Separate scope change from schedule change.
- Distinguish owner delay from dependency or approval delay.
- Do not recommend rebaselining merely to eliminate unfavorable variance.
- Clearly separate current status from recommended action.

## Quality standards

A strong deliverable-tracking output must be:

- Defined: each deliverable has a clear expected output or outcome.
- Owned: the delivery owner and accountable approver are identified.
- Time-bound: original and current dates are preserved.
- Evidence-based: progress and completion are verifiable.
- Acceptance-aware: delivery and formal acceptance are distinguished.
- Dependency-aware: upstream and downstream effects are visible.
- Comparable: current performance can be evaluated against the baseline.
- Current: status is tied to a reporting cutoff date.
- Actionable: next steps and requested decisions are clear.
- Traceable: scope, schedule, status, and approval changes have sources.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not invent deliverables, owners, approvers, dates, progress, acceptance, or completion.
- Do not automatically modify scope, ownership, status, or schedules.
- Do not treat generated status as formal approval.
- Do not conceal schedule or scope variance.
- Do not make personnel or performance judgments.
- Do not expose confidential financial, personnel, clinical, legal, security, or privacy information to unauthorized audiences.
- Flag stale, missing, conflicting, or unsupported information.
- Require authorized human review before closure, escalation, cancellation, or rebaselining.

## Human role

The deliverable owner, program lead, accountable approver, sponsor, or executive must:

- Confirm scope and acceptance criteria.
- Validate ownership and due dates.
- Confirm progress and forecast information.
- Approve scope or schedule changes.
- Resolve priority and resource conflicts.
- Accept or reject the deliverable.
- Authorize implementation and closure.
- Approve escalation, rebaselining, deferral, cancellation, or reassignment.
