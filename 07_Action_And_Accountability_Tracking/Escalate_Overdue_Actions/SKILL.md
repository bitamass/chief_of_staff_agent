# Skill: Escalate Overdue Actions

## Purpose

This skill identifies overdue actions, evaluates their impact and urgency, and recommends a proportionate escalation path.

It helps leadership determine:

- Which overdue actions require attention.
- Why an action remains incomplete.
- What decisions, milestones, or outcomes are affected.
- Whether the owner needs a reminder, assistance, sponsor intervention, or executive escalation.
- What response or recovery commitment is required.
- Whether repeated delays indicate a systemic delivery or accountability issue.

The skill supports human-led accountability. It must not automatically send escalations, reassign work, alter deadlines, or make personnel judgments.

## Core activities

### 1. Validate the action record

Before recommending escalation:

- Confirm the action description.
- Confirm the owner and accountable leader.
- Confirm the original and current due dates.
- Verify that the action has not already been completed, cancelled, superseded, or formally deferred.
- Identify the latest reliable status update.
- Confirm the source of the commitment.
- Flag missing or conflicting information.

An action must not be treated as overdue solely because an outdated tracker has not been updated.

### 2. Calculate overdue status

Determine:

- Days overdue.
- Number of revised deadlines.
- Time since the last owner update.
- Time remaining before an affected milestone or decision.
- Whether the action is on the critical path.
- Whether another action or deliverable depends on it.

Report calendar days unless the governing process explicitly requires business days.

### 3. Assess impact

Evaluate documented impact across relevant dimensions:

- Executive decision readiness.
- Delivery schedule.
- Operational continuity.
- Financial outcome.
- Clinical or patient-related operations.
- Technology or integration readiness.
- Security, privacy, legal, regulatory, or compliance obligations.
- Stakeholder confidence.
- Reputation.
- Dependency on another team or vendor.

Do not assign high severity merely because an action is old. Severity should reflect consequence, while urgency reflects required response timing.

### 4. Review follow-up history

Determine:

- Whether the owner received a clear assignment.
- Whether the owner acknowledged the commitment.
- Whether reminders were sent.
- Whether blockers were documented.
- Whether assistance or a decision was requested.
- Whether the deadline was previously revised.
- Whether prior escalation occurred.
- Whether the owner has provided a credible recovery plan.

Lack of completion caused by an unresolved executive decision should not be characterized as owner nonperformance.

### 5. Select a proportionate escalation level

Use documented policy when available. Otherwise, recommend:

#### Monitor

Use when:

- The action is minimally overdue.
- Impact is low.
- A credible completion date exists.
- No dependent decision or milestone is at risk.

#### Reminder required

Use when:

- The due date has passed.
- The owner has not supplied an updated status.
- Impact remains limited.
- A direct reminder is likely sufficient.

#### Owner follow-up required

Use when:

- The action is materially overdue.
- The blocker or recovery plan is unclear.
- Dependencies are beginning to be affected.
- A specific response and revised commitment are needed.

#### Sponsor attention required

Use when:

- The action affects a significant milestone or decision.
- The owner lacks authority or resources to resolve the blocker.
- Multiple deadlines have been missed.
- Cross-team intervention is required.

#### Executive escalation required

Use when:

- Continued delay threatens a strategic commitment, material investment, regulatory requirement, major operational outcome, or executive decision.
- Previous escalation has not resolved the issue.
- Ownership or priority conflict requires executive authority.

#### Immediate risk or governance escalation

Use when:

- Delay may create material safety, privacy, security, legal, regulatory, clinical, financial, or continuity exposure.
- Required governance or assurance work is incomplete.
- The escalation must follow a specialized incident, risk, or compliance process.

### 6. Recommend a resolution

Every escalation recommendation should specify:

- What is overdue.
- Why it matters.
- What has already been attempted.
- The documented blocker.
- The person or role who can resolve it.
- The decision, resource, clarification, or commitment required.
- The required response date.
- The proposed recovery date.
- The next escalation step if unresolved.

Escalation should seek resolution, not merely draw attention to failure.

### 7. Identify systemic patterns

Across multiple actions, detect:

- Repeated overdue assignments by initiative or function.
- Chronic dependency delays.
- Unclear responsibility.
- Missing decision authority.
- Unrealistic due dates.
- Resource constraints.
- Excessive action volume.
- Weak closure criteria.
- Repeated deadline changes without documented approval.
- Actions remaining open after their underlying need has disappeared.

## Decision rules

- Verify that the action is genuinely open before escalating.
- Distinguish owner delay from dependency, resource, or decision delay.
- Separate severity from urgency.
- Consider business impact, not only days overdue.
- Escalate to the lowest organizational level able to resolve the issue.
- Do not escalate directly to an executive when an owner or sponsor can resolve it.
- Do not characterize an owner as unresponsive without documented follow-up attempts.
- Do not use escalation as punishment.
- Do not recommend reassignment without appropriate authority.
- Clearly separate facts, documented explanations, and recommendations.
- Include a specific requested resolution and response date.

## Quality standards

A strong escalation must be:

- Accurate: status, ownership, and dates are verified.
- Proportionate: escalation level reflects impact and urgency.
- Evidence-based: claims connect to source records.
- Resolution-oriented: the communication requests a concrete intervention.
- Fair: it accounts for documented blockers and dependencies.
- Timely: escalation occurs before avoidable consequences increase.
- Concise: the recipient can quickly understand the issue.
- Traceable: prior follow-ups and decisions are documented.
- Reviewable: a human can approve or revise the recommendation.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not automatically send an escalation.
- Do not change an owner, due date, priority, or status.
- Do not make performance, disciplinary, or character judgments.
- Do not infer negligence, resistance, or lack of commitment.
- Do not disclose sensitive personnel or confidential information unnecessarily.
- Do not bypass required management, HR, legal, compliance, or governance processes.
- Do not create an artificial deadline without human authorization.
- Flag missing or unreliable action data.
- Require human approval before any escalation is communicated.

## Human role

The action owner, program lead, sponsor, or executive must:

- Validate that the action remains required and overdue.
- Confirm the current owner and blocker.
- Approve the escalation level and recipient.
- Determine whether assistance, reprioritization, reassignment, or deadline revision is appropriate.
- Authorize any communication.
- Resolve conflicts involving resources, ownership, or priorities.
- Confirm when the action can be closed or de-escalated.
