# Skill: Monitor Commitments

## Purpose

This skill creates and maintains a source-linked view of commitments made by executives, leaders, teams, and stakeholders.

It helps leadership determine:

- What was promised.
- Who made and owns each commitment.
- To whom the commitment was made.
- When it is due.
- Whether it remains on track.
- What evidence is required for closure.
- Which commitments require follow-up, assistance, renegotiation, or escalation.
- Whether repeated commitment failures reveal broader delivery or accountability problems.

A commitment is more than a general action item. It represents an explicit promise, agreement, obligation, or undertaking made to another person, group, governing body, or initiative.

## Core activities

### 1. Identify commitments

Extract commitments from approved:

- Meeting notes and minutes.
- Decisions.
- Executive communications.
- Stakeholder correspondence.
- Initiative charters.
- Action trackers.
- Governance records.
- Agreements and plans.
- Status reports.

A valid commitment should contain, or be capable of being validated against:

- A specific expected outcome.
- A responsible or accountable party.
- A recipient or beneficiary.
- A due date or timing expectation.
- A source.
- Completion or acceptance criteria.

Do not treat general intentions, discussion points, suggestions, or aspirations as confirmed commitments.

### 2. Normalize the commitment record

For each commitment:

- Rewrite the commitment as a clear deliverable or outcome without changing its meaning.
- Preserve the original wording as source evidence where appropriate.
- Identify the commitment maker, owner, recipient, due date, and source.
- Connect the commitment to the relevant initiative, decision, meeting, or stakeholder.
- Identify completion and acceptance criteria.
- Flag any missing field for human validation.

### 3. Detect duplicates and relationships

Identify:

- The same commitment recorded in multiple sources.
- Commitments that overlap or conflict.
- Commitments that depend on other commitments.
- Commitments that were replaced or superseded.
- Parent commitments with multiple supporting actions.
- Separate actions incorrectly combined into one commitment.

Do not merge records unless the evidence shows they represent the same obligation.

### 4. Assess current status

Evaluate:

- Progress evidence.
- Time remaining.
- Current forecast.
- Dependencies and blockers.
- Prior follow-up.
- Impact of delay.
- Whether the owner still has authority, capacity, and required inputs.
- Whether the expected outcome remains relevant.
- Whether acceptance is still required.

Status should reflect both delivery progress and the likelihood of meeting the commitment.

### 5. Validate completion

A commitment should be marked Completed only when:

- The promised outcome has been delivered.
- Applicable completion criteria have been met.
- Required approval or acceptance has been obtained.
- Any necessary documentation exists.
- The authorized owner or recipient confirms closure when required.

“Work submitted,” “draft completed,” or “owner reports complete” may not be sufficient if approval or acceptance remains outstanding. Use Pending acceptance when appropriate.

### 6. Monitor due dates and changes

Track:

- Commitments approaching their due dates.
- Overdue commitments.
- Changes to ownership, timing, scope, or expected outcomes.
- Revised deadlines and who approved them.
- Commitments deferred, cancelled, or superseded.
- Repeated changes without documented agreement.

Changing a date in a tracker does not, by itself, amend the original commitment. Preserve both the original and current due date.

### 7. Recommend follow-up

For each material commitment, recommend:

- No action.
- Routine monitoring.
- Owner status request.
- Dependency resolution.
- Recovery-plan confirmation.
- Recipient communication.
- Sponsor support.
- Renegotiation.
- Escalation.
- Formal closure.

Every recommendation should identify the responsible person, desired response, and timing.

### 8. Identify systemic patterns

Across the commitment portfolio, analyze:

- Recurring missed commitments.
- Owners with excessive commitment load.
- Chronic dependency problems.
- Commitments made without resources or decision authority.
- Vague commitments without acceptance criteria.
- Repeated deadline changes.
- Commitments that remain open despite no longer being relevant.
- Stakeholders awaiting multiple unresolved promises.
- Gaps between executive decisions and operational follow-through.

## Decision rules

- Do not classify a statement as a commitment unless the source indicates an explicit obligation, promise, agreement, or assignment.
- Preserve the original source and wording.
- Distinguish the commitment maker from the person responsible for delivery.
- Distinguish the accountable owner from supporting contributors.
- Preserve the original due date when a new date is approved.
- Do not mark a commitment complete without evidence.
- Use Pending acceptance when delivery occurred but required acceptance remains outstanding.
- Do not assume silence from the recipient means acceptance.
- Do not interpret lack of progress as owner negligence.
- Distinguish owner delay from unresolved dependencies or decisions.
- Do not cancel, defer, reassign, or renegotiate a commitment without authorization.
- Link escalations to consequence and prior follow-up, not age alone.

## Quality standards

A strong commitment-monitoring output must be:

- Complete: material commitments are captured.
- Specific: the promised outcome is clear.
- Attributable: the maker, owner, and recipient are identified.
- Time-bound: original and current due dates are visible.
- Evidence-based: progress and completion connect to sources.
- Acceptance-aware: delivery and acceptance are distinguished.
- Current: every active commitment has a recent status or is flagged.
- Traceable: changes, reminders, and escalations are recorded.
- Actionable: the next follow-up is clear.
- Fair: blockers and dependencies are represented accurately.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not invent commitments, owners, due dates, acceptance criteria, or stakeholder expectations.
- Do not automatically send reminders or escalations.
- Do not change commitment terms, ownership, or timing.
- Do not make performance or disciplinary judgments.
- Do not expose confidential, privileged, personnel, clinical, legal, security, or privacy information to unauthorized audiences.
- Do not treat the generated register as an official agreement unless approved.
- Flag ambiguous, contradictory, stale, or unsupported records.
- Require human review before consequential follow-up or escalation.

## Human role

The commitment owner, recipient, initiative lead, sponsor, or executive must:

- Confirm that the commitment was accurately captured.
- Validate ownership, recipient, timing, and expected outcome.
- Confirm progress and blockers.
- Approve revisions to scope or due dates.
- Determine whether escalation or renegotiation is appropriate.
- Confirm delivery and acceptance.
- Authorize closure, cancellation, deferral, reassignment, or supersession.
