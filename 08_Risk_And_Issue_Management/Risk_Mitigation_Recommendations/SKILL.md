# Skill: Risk Mitigation Recommendations

## Purpose

This skill develops and compares proportionate options for reducing, transferring, avoiding, monitoring, or accepting a documented risk.

It helps leadership determine:

- What exposure exists.
- What controls are already in place.
- Whether those controls are effective.
- What additional mitigation options are feasible.
- How each option changes probability, impact, cost, timing, and residual risk.
- Which option best fits organizational objectives and risk tolerance.
- What decisions, resources, approvals, and monitoring are required.

The skill recommends options; it does not accept risk, authorize spending, change controls, initiate an incident response, or approve implementation.

## Core risk-response strategies

### Avoid

Remove the activity, condition, design, or dependency that creates the risk.

Use when:

- The exposure exceeds tolerance.
- Mitigation is impractical or ineffective.
- The activity is not essential.
- A safer viable alternative exists.

Avoidance may also eliminate benefits or delay objectives. These trade-offs must be stated.

### Reduce probability

Decrease the likelihood that the event will occur.

Examples include:

- Preventive controls.
- Process redesign.
- Access restrictions.
- Testing and validation.
- Training.
- Redundancy.
- Vendor or dependency changes.
- Earlier review or approval.

### Reduce impact

Limit the consequence if the event occurs.

Examples include:

- Containment.
- Segmentation.
- Backup and recovery.
- Continuity planning.
- Phased implementation.
- Rollback capability.
- Financial reserves.
- Manual fallback procedures.

### Transfer or share

Move or distribute some consequences or responsibilities through:

- Insurance.
- Contractual allocation.
- Vendor commitments.
- Partnerships.
- Shared services.
- Indemnification or warranties, when authorized.

Transfer does not necessarily eliminate operational, reputational, regulatory, or accountability exposure.

### Accept with monitoring

Formally retain the residual risk when:

- Exposure is within tolerance.
- Further mitigation is disproportionate.
- The benefit justifies the risk.
- The authorized decision-maker accepts it.
- Monitoring and contingency arrangements are sufficient.

The skill may recommend consideration of acceptance but must not approve it.

### Prepare contingency or recovery

Establish a response if the risk materializes.

A contingency plan complements preventive mitigation; it does not reduce probability unless it also changes the conditions creating the risk.

## Core activities

### 1. Validate the risk

Before recommending mitigation:

- Confirm that the item is a future uncertainty rather than a current issue.
- Confirm the cause, event, and impact.
- Identify the affected objective.
- Verify the owner.
- Review probability, impact, velocity, detectability, and time horizon.
- Confirm the source and current status.
- Identify material assumptions and uncertainty.

If the event has already occurred, route the matter to issue management, containment, or incident response as appropriate.

### 2. Evaluate current controls

For each control:

- Confirm that the control exists.
- Identify the owner.
- Determine its purpose.
- Review implementation and coverage.
- Examine evidence of operation.
- Assess effectiveness.
- Identify gaps and limitations.

Do not assume a documented policy, planned control, or untested process is operating effectively.

### 3. Establish current exposure

Distinguish:

- Inherent risk.
- Control effectiveness.
- Residual risk.
- Risk appetite.
- Risk tolerance.
- Current trend.
- Time available to act.

Preserve the owner’s assessment while clearly showing any alternative analytical assessment and its evidence.

### 4. Generate viable options

Develop materially different responses rather than minor variations of one approach.

For each option:

- Identify the response strategy.
- Explain how it affects cause, probability, or impact.
- Identify required actions.
- Estimate time, cost, resources, and dependencies.
- Identify governance and approval needs.
- Identify secondary risks.
- Estimate residual exposure.
- State assumptions and confidence.

Include a reasonable no-additional-action or accept-and-monitor option when appropriate so leadership can compare proportionality.

### 5. Evaluate proportionality

Assess whether each option’s:

- Cost.
- Complexity.
- Delivery time.
- Operational burden.
- Opportunity cost.
- Secondary risk.

is proportionate to:

- Expected risk reduction.
- Severity of potential impact.
- Time horizon.
- Legal or regulatory obligation.
- Organizational risk tolerance.
- Strategic value of the underlying activity.

Low-cost exposure must not automatically receive expensive mitigation unless a mandatory requirement or catastrophic consequence justifies it.

### 6. Compare trade-offs

Compare options using consistent criteria:

- Risk reduction.
- Cost.
- Speed.
- Feasibility.
- Governance fit.
- Reversibility.
- Stakeholder effect.
- Dependency complexity.
- Long-term sustainability.
- Confidence in effectiveness.

Do not present subjective numerical scores as objective facts unless the scoring method is documented.

### 7. Recommend a response

The recommendation should:

- Address the most important cause or consequence.
- Fit within constraints.
- Identify clear ownership.
- Provide implementation sequencing.
- Include success measures.
- Define residual-risk monitoring.
- Identify required approvals.
- State residual risk requiring acceptance.
- Include contingency planning when warranted.

### 8. Define validation and monitoring

Mitigation is not complete merely because an action was implemented.

Define:

- Evidence of implementation.
- Evidence of effectiveness.
- Key risk indicators.
- Control-performance measures.
- Review cadence.
- Trigger thresholds.
- Conditions for reassessment.
- Conditions requiring escalation.

### 9. Address residual risk

After mitigation:

- Estimate remaining exposure.
- Compare it with tolerance.
- Identify unresolved uncertainty.
- Determine whether additional treatment is required.
- Identify the person or body authorized to accept the risk.
- Establish monitoring and review requirements.

## Decision rules

- Verify the risk before recommending treatment.
- Distinguish planned controls from implemented controls.
- Distinguish implementation from proven effectiveness.
- Do not reduce a risk rating solely because mitigation is planned.
- Prefer options that address root causes when feasible.
- Consider both probability reduction and impact reduction.
- Identify secondary risks created by mitigation.
- Consider a no-action or accept-and-monitor option when legally and operationally permissible.
- Do not recommend risk acceptance without identifying the authorized decision-maker.
- Do not assume risk transfer eliminates institutional accountability.
- Do not recommend disproportionate controls without explaining why.
- Clearly separate facts, assumptions, estimates, and recommendations.
- Route current incidents or active harm to the appropriate response process.

## Quality standards

A strong mitigation recommendation must be:

- Risk-specific: it addresses the documented cause, event, or impact.
- Evidence-based: control and mitigation assessments are supported.
- Comparative: leadership can understand alternatives and trade-offs.
- Proportionate: expected benefit justifies cost and complexity.
- Feasible: ownership, resources, dependencies, and timing are addressed.
- Measurable: effectiveness can be evaluated.
- Residual-risk aware: remaining exposure is visible.
- Governed: approvals and acceptance authority are identified.
- Transparent: assumptions and uncertainty are clear.
- Actionable: the next decision and implementation steps are explicit.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not fabricate risk estimates, control effectiveness, costs, benefits, or residual-risk ratings.
- Do not make definitive legal, clinical, security, privacy, financial, regulatory, or compliance determinations.
- Do not automatically implement controls or mitigation actions.
- Do not authorize spending, procurement, staffing, or risk acceptance.
- Do not replace formal architecture, assurance, incident-response, audit, or governance review.
- Do not conceal secondary risks or material trade-offs.
- Do not expose restricted risk information to unauthorized audiences.
- Flag missing evidence, conflicting assessments, and low-confidence estimates.
- Require specialized and human review when the risk involves consequential institutional exposure.

## Human role

The risk owner, control owner, initiative lead, specialized assurance partner, governance body, or authorized executive must:

- Validate the risk statement and assessment.
- Confirm control effectiveness.
- Review feasibility and cost.
- Approve the selected response.
- Authorize funding and implementation.
- Determine whether specialized review is required.
- Accept residual risk when authorized.
- Monitor effectiveness and approve closure or reassessment.
