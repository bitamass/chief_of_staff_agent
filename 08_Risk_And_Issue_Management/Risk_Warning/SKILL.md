# Skill: Risk Warning

## Purpose

This skill monitors approved information for signals that a known or emerging risk may be increasing, approaching realization, or requiring earlier leadership attention.

It helps leadership determine:

- What has changed.
- Whether a threshold has been crossed.
- Whether the change is isolated or persistent.
- Which risk, objective, decision, or operation may be affected.
- How quickly the exposure may develop.
- Whether current controls and mitigations remain sufficient.
- What validation, monitoring, mitigation, contingency, or escalation should occur.

The skill provides warnings for human review. It does not declare emergencies, initiate incidents, notify stakeholders, activate contingency plans, or make formal risk decisions automatically.

## Core concepts

### Indicator

A measurable or observable signal associated with risk exposure.

An indicator may be:

- Quantitative, such as schedule variance or error rate.
- Qualitative, such as repeated stakeholder concerns.
- Leading, signaling possible future exposure.
- Lagging, confirming that deterioration or harm has already occurred.

### Trigger

A defined condition that changes the monitoring or response state.

Examples:

- A metric crossing a threshold.
- A milestone becoming likely to slip.
- A control failing.
- Multiple related concerns appearing.
- A mitigation action becoming materially overdue.
- A decision remaining unresolved beyond a critical date.

### Warning

A documented notification that available evidence indicates increased exposure or a need for review.

A warning is not proof that the risk will occur.

### Risk realization

The point at which the uncertain event occurs. Once this happens, the matter may require issue or incident management instead of continued risk warning.

## Warning levels

Use approved organizational definitions when available. Otherwise, the following may be used as clearly labeled demonstration guidance.

### Informational

Use when:

- A change is relevant but remains within expected limits.
- No immediate response is required.
- Continued routine monitoring is appropriate.

### Watch

Use when:

- An indicator is approaching a threshold.
- Early evidence suggests deterioration.
- The signal is not yet sufficiently persistent or material for escalation.
- Validation or more frequent monitoring is appropriate.

### Warning

Use when:

- A defined threshold has been crossed.
- Multiple credible signals point to increasing exposure.
- A mitigation, decision, or dependency is unlikely to be resolved in time.
- Management response is required.

### Critical

Use when:

- Exposure may materialize rapidly.
- A material objective, obligation, service, safety condition, or institutional interest may be affected.
- Immediate specialized, governance, or executive review is required.
- A contingency or incident-response process may need activation.

The skill may recommend a critical warning but must not declare an official emergency unless authorized.

## Core activities

### 1. Define the monitoring frame

- Identify the objectives and risks being monitored.
- Confirm approved indicators and thresholds.
- Establish the data cutoff time.
- Confirm monitoring frequency.
- Identify owners and required recipients.
- Confirm escalation and specialized response processes.

Do not monitor undefined objectives with arbitrary thresholds.

### 2. Validate monitoring data

For each signal:

- Confirm the source.
- Confirm the timestamp.
- Check completeness and timeliness.
- Identify known outages or anomalies.
- Compare conflicting sources.
- Assess confidence.
- Flag signals requiring validation.

A missing update must not automatically be interpreted as deterioration. It may be a data-quality problem.

### 3. Detect threshold conditions

Evaluate:

- Current value.
- Baseline.
- Expected range.
- Threshold.
- Direction and rate of change.
- Duration.
- Persistence.
- Frequency.
- Correlation with other indicators.

Avoid issuing material warnings from a single transient variation unless the potential consequence or velocity warrants immediate review.

### 4. Evaluate context

Before recommending a warning level, consider:

- Current risk rating.
- Risk velocity.
- Detectability.
- Existing controls.
- Mitigation status.
- Time remaining before a decision or milestone.
- Related risks and issues.
- Historical patterns.
- Seasonal or operational variation.
- External changes.
- Alternative explanations.

Threshold crossing should prompt analysis, not automatic conclusions.

### 5. Corroborate signals

Strengthen confidence by comparing:

- Quantitative metrics.
- Meeting and stakeholder observations.
- Status reports.
- Control results.
- Action and decision delays.
- Historical patterns.
- Related risks or issues.
- External evidence, when authorized.

Clearly label warnings based on a single source or weak evidence.

### 6. Distinguish risk warning from issue detection

Determine whether:

- Exposure is increasing but the event remains uncertain.
- The risk event has occurred.
- An issue already exists.
- A specialized incident process should be engaged.

If the event has occurred, recommend transition to issue or incident management rather than continuing to describe it only as a warning.

### 7. Recommend a proportionate response

Possible recommendations include:

- Validate the data.
- Increase monitoring frequency.
- Contact the risk owner.
- Accelerate mitigation.
- Resolve a dependency.
- Obtain a pending decision.
- Test a control.
- Prepare a contingency.
- Notify a governance or assurance function.
- Escalate to a sponsor or executive.
- Initiate the appropriate issue or incident process.

State the owner, action, timing, and escalation condition.

### 8. Manage warning lifecycle

Track warnings through:

- Detection.
- Validation.
- Assignment.
- Response.
- Escalation.
- De-escalation.
- Conversion to issue or incident.
- Closure.

A warning should not remain open indefinitely without review.

### 9. Learn from warning performance

Review:

- False positives.
- Missed warnings.
- Late warnings.
- Threshold sensitivity.
- Data-source reliability.
- Response timeliness.
- Whether recommended actions reduced exposure.
- Whether new indicators or thresholds are needed.

Changes to official thresholds require authorized review and approval.

## Decision rules

- Use approved indicators and thresholds when available.
- Define the time period and data cutoff.
- Do not interpret missing data automatically as adverse performance.
- Do not issue a high-severity warning from an isolated signal without considering context, unless velocity or consequence justifies immediate review.
- Distinguish threshold crossing from confirmed risk realization.
- Distinguish risk warnings from active issues and incidents.
- Consider direction, rate, persistence, and corroboration—not only current value.
- Show alternative explanations.
- Label confidence clearly.
- Do not automatically escalate, notify, or activate contingency plans.
- Do not change official thresholds without authorization.
- Preserve the full warning history, including de-escalation and false-positive disposition.

## Quality standards

A strong risk warning must be:

- Timely.
- Evidence-based.
- Contextual.
- Proportionate.
- Explainable.
- Confidence-aware.
- Actionable.
- Traceable.
- Lifecycle-managed.
- Reviewable by an accountable human.

The recipient should understand what changed, why it matters, how reliable the signal is, and what should happen next.

## Safeguards and limitations

- Do not fabricate signals, thresholds, risk relationships, or impacts.
- Do not perform unauthorized surveillance or collect restricted information.
- Do not make definitive legal, clinical, cybersecurity, privacy, regulatory, financial, or safety determinations.
- Do not automatically contact stakeholders or external authorities.
- Do not declare an official incident or emergency.
- Do not activate contingency, recovery, or incident-response procedures.
- Do not expose sensitive warning information to unauthorized audiences.
- Avoid alarmist language unsupported by evidence.
- Flag incomplete, stale, conflicting, or low-confidence data.
- Require human and specialized review for consequential warnings.

## Human role

The risk owner, monitoring owner, program lead, specialized reviewer, governance function, sponsor, or executive must:

- Validate the signal.
- Confirm the warning level.
- Approve escalation and notification.
- Determine whether the risk has become an issue or incident.
- Authorize mitigation or contingency action.
- Review false positives and monitoring gaps.
- Approve threshold changes.
- Confirm de-escalation or closure.
